Preface

Statue of Ganesha of Singasari from 13th century, collection of National Museum, Jakarta Indonesia. Photo by Bagoes Wiryomartono.

This book is a collection of essays on Javanese culture that describes, analyzes, and discusses the manifold features and characteristics of Javanese culture and society comprising aesthetics, the art of living, urban settlement, and political culture. As a whole, the book is searching for the meanings of locality in the context of arts, architecture, polity, and society. It argues that study on Java is a significant contribution to counteract the contemporary global culture; for several centuries, Javanese tradition has been graciously synergizing and integrating foreign influences into its sociocultural system in the sense of syncretism. By exploring the identities and differences of Javanese culture we are able to enrich and enhance our understanding of the importance of diversity. In doing so, globalization does not mean the colonialization of monoculture modernity, but the transmission of multiplicity and intricacy of various cultural sources.

The specific mission of this book is to strengthen cross-disciplinary studies on sustainable culture and societies in Southeast Asia, especially in dealing with their vibrant diversity, economic growth, global communication, and environmental crisis. The data and materials of the study are based on the author’s life experience as a native Javanese growing up in Javanese tradition. Until recently, mostly non-Javanese scholars or Western academicians have written scholarly studies and treatments on Javanese culture and tradition. We seldom hear and read studies on Javanese culture and tradition from a Javanese perspective. This book is the author’s considerable recollection and critical reflection as a Javanese person about his Javanese world and society. The purpose of this publication is to dismantle and unmask the relationship between local concepts and phenomena of culture that hold up and sustain the hegemony of Javanese influence upon Indonesian society.

The publication of this book is expected to renew, enrich, and enhance the debates and discussions on locality in dealing with global issues in terms of knowledge, ethics, and aesthetics. Undergraduate and graduate students of humanities and social sciences could be the captive market of this publication. Specialists and scholars on Asian studies will be interested in reading this book for deepening and widening a discourse on the importance of local concepts. Furthermore, this book is an effective material of reflection for most scholars and policymakers in Indonesia in understanding their culture and tradition.

The search for the components of Javanese culture in this book is focused on the examinations and expositions of native or local concepts concerning arts, domain, society, polity, custom, tradition, and culture. The sources of data for the study are mainly Javanese texts, spoken language, and collected works in various textual forms, including from dictionaries, Javanese inscriptions, and special collections.

Chapter One

Aesthetics in Javanese Culture and Tradition

What is aesthetics in Javanese culture? Why are aesthetics and ethics in Javanese society interchangeable? This chapter attempts to deal with these questions. In order to understand the relationship between aesthetics and ethics in Javanese tradition, this chapter argues that both have the same foundation and function in Javanese culture and society. In order to do so, the chapter applies a hermeneutical analysis that examines and scrutinizes the meanings of local concepts and their phenomena in works of art. The purpose is to unfold the thought and sensibility of Javanese concepts in dealing with art and beauty. Concepts and phenomena related to aesthetic experience will be examined, scrutinized, and unfolded to divulge the reality of the Javanese world.

Javanese Culture in the Indonesian World

Even though Javanese culture is geographically established in Central Java, in which Surakarta and Yogyakarta are the centers, its cultural influence in Indonesia is widespread and predominant. Concerning its dominant populations, the Javanese way of life plays an important role in Indonesian politics and culture. Since aesthetics in society is about ideas, tastes, information, and expressions, studying its phenomena and reality is one of the most effective ways to understand the country’s way of life and its values.

A number of scholars (Richter 1994; Jessup 1990; Holt 1967) have presented studies on the arts of Java as part of Indonesian culture. Their works have explored forms, styles, meanings, and historical aspects of arts and crafts in Indonesia that enable us to go further into the question of aesthetics. Indeed, Javanese arts are indivisible from its customs and traditions that nurture and sustain its presence in its society. In this respect, Javanese arts and aesthetics show their capacity as an integrated part of Indonesian culture, which has been studied profoundly by many scholars (Anderson 2006; Beatty 1999; Sears 1996; Florida 1995; Pamberton 1994; Hefner 1985; van Groenendael 1985; and Geertz 1960). Their works not only show us many insightful aspects of the Javanese culture, but also show that there are many things to be done for further investigations. Among these is the Javanese sense of aesthetics, which has been so far short of coverage. As with many other cultures and traditions, Javanese culture has its specific and particular intentionality that depicts and demonstrates its historical evolution and contextual innovation.

Even though aesthetics is an indispensable outcome of emotion, expression, impression, taste, and feeling, aesthetic experience is, in many ways, inseparable from personal judgment and sentiment as well as part of contextual collective values. However, in social and practical context, aesthetic matters are mostly conditioned by local culture and tradition, in terms of codes, manners, customs, and norms.

Ostensibly observed, showing feeling in Javanese tradition is a shameful conduct or a sign of weakness, even a sin against God (Geertz 1960). Aesthetics in Javanese tradition is likely indivisible from ethics—the value and judgment of good or bad as well as beauty or ugliness have something to do with courtesy and manner. Accordingly, the Javanese tradition seemingly does give a room for common people to express their feelings and thoughts. As a matter of fact, in their daily world, the Javanese people are aptly to uphold self-control for gestures, mimics, and body language with emotional expression or sentimental phrases; this includes handling emotional reactions and anger with perseverance. Despite various turbulences and crises—ranging from politics, to the economy, to the environment—Javanese people are taught by their tradition and culture to be tough and compassionate without showing any emotion or affection. All of this is not expected to happen overnight, but in childhood intensive self-training begins that involves celibacy, fasting, meditation, and other restraints such as from certain food, habits, and pleasures.

As a branch of philosophy for the experience of feeling, aesthetics in Javanese culture is challenged by the question of ethics. In some ways, Javanese aesthetics is probably not too much about bodily pleasure, but more about well-being and good manners. This implies that ethics and aesthetics in Javanese tradition are interchangeable (Hughes-Freeland 2008, p. 83).

This chapter investigates the cultural concepts that represent and work within Javanese culture for growth, beauty, and sustainability. The purpose is to explore Javanese cultural forces and mechanisms that have been resiliently maintaining and developing domination and hegemony over Indonesian political culture since 1945. This chapter argues that the concept of beauty does not simply affect the definition of a cultural identity and boundary, but also aesthetically in cultivating the sense of being Javanese in peaceful presence. Everything old and new in the world is subject to harmonization toward sustainability, as articulated in the Javanese credo: laras yektine lestari.

Why are ethics and aesthetics in Javanese tradition indistinguishable? This chapter will deal with the question and argues that both civility and the sense of beauty in Javanese culture have the same source and purpose. In order to understand this, the chapter will examine and scrutinize the relationship between local concepts and its phenomena in various works of art, which are related to and associated with Javanese cultural values and customs.

The Javanese World: Bhawana

Semaoen probably presents one of the best descriptions of the Javanese world in the modern age in his novel Hikayat Kadiroen, the story of Kadiroen, in the chapter “Terjepit” (“Being Squeezed”). The novel was written in 1919 when Semaoen was jailed by the Dutch colonial power because of persdelict on his essay in Sinar Hindia, containing sympathetic support of the Indonesian nationalist movement (Semaoen 2000; Maier 2004, p. 171). According to Semaoen’s depiction in his only novel, the modern Javanese world has lost its spiritually ordering power. Indeed, as a Javanese person, he is not alone. Previously, Javanese populations pertained their cultural destiny and nation to the prophetic messages of the Kediri Third King, Jayabaya (1135–1157). In the nineteenth century, an influential Javanese poet, R. Ng. Ranggawarsita, told us a similar depiction of the world in his famous poem “Serat Kalatida,” or message from the Dark Age (jaman kalabendu). The prophetic message of Ranggawarsita that was believed to be the destiny of Javanese culture still lingers in Javanese populations; the relationship between his message and his personal life remains mysterious and influential today (Andjar 1980; Andjar 1979). Despite the infiltration of the Dutch language and Javanology, Takashi Shiraishi and Andjar Any as quoted by Tsuchiya come to the conclusion that Ranggawarsita and his world continue to live on in the world of the Javanese language (Tschuchiya 1990, p. 108). It is a typically Javanese way of thinking of the world that the heavenly flourishing world is always in the past or in the future, but it hardly deals with the immediate scene of the present.

In contrast to the past realm, the contemporary world is depicted as hell with socially degrading values, spiritually swallowing resources, politically disintegrating leadership, and environmentally triggered disasters. All of these are obviously supportive of the justification of the patrimonial and prophetic messages of those who are not in power at the time because of their own choice or still in struggle for that. In many ways, the perfect Javanese world is never in present times, but it is high above the horizon of the common people’s understanding and is believed to come true someday as the realization of the messianic promise of Javanese myths, legends, poems, and stories since the Stanza of the King Jayabaya from the twelfth century (Florida 1995, p. 273). Most recently, in March 2008, the prominent Javanese Indonesian poet W. S. Rendra in his inauguration speech as Doctor Honoris Causa at the University of Gadjahmada depicted the contemporary world in the Age of Darkness (kalatida-kalabendu), toward the Golden Age (kalasuba) (Kolese De Britto Yogyakarta 2008). As a poet he affirms that his thought on the world is in alignment with his predecessor, Ranggawarsita.

The idea of the Javanese world is likely indivisible from the myth of murwakala (the origin of time), which is commonly used for the opening part of a shadow puppet show (wayang). The origin of kala is perceived by the Javanese as the beginning of an era that has been liberated from chaos, famine, impurity, disorder, and suffering. The Javanese world, bhawana or jagat, is believed to be born from this darkness that is known as suker, derived from the Sanskrit word sukerta, meaning anguish, agony, and torment (Headley 2000, p. 158). In Javanese tradition, kala or time is depicted as a monster that is always ready to eat everything including humans, animals, and plants.

Indeed, the birth of kala in the Javanese myth does not have to do with any anniversary or special event, but simply to remind people of the perishability of the world that is always in need of ruwatan. Ruwatan is derived from the word ruwat, meaning to liberate from perishability with care for sustainability. The meanings of ruwatan are indivisible from the Javanese rites of slametan. Clifford Geertz has described in detail how the slametan plays an important role in Javanese rites of passage (Geertz 1960, pp. 77–85). His description is still valid today, especially in rural areas of Central and East Java. Accordingly, the rituals are dedicated to the care of unity (manunggal), harmony (rukun), and of sustainability (lestari) of the world with the acceptance (nrima) of the destiny of mortality.

In the Javanese myth of the world creation, time as sign and power of perishability is depicted as the son of the Batara Guru and Batari Uma. Even though his character in Javanese tradition is monstrous, rude, bluntly honest, indifferent, and indiscriminately savage to any being, Batara Kala never does violence with bloodshed. The necessity for ruwatan is more about anticipatory care for the well-being, unity, and harmony of the world. There are several kinds of the rite of murwakala including for personal well-being (anggara), for communal well-being (mageri), and for avoiding natural disaster (sengkala). The world in Javanese cosmogony is perceived as the realm of beings with the constant threat of perishability and transience that invokes the necessity for continuous awareness of care for well-being (slamet) that includes unity (manunggal), harmony (rukun), and sustainability (lestari).

The puppet master (dhalang) plays an important role as priest. The priest enunciates and leads the ruwatan with wayang performance. The Javanese people—from the west to the east part of the island—have practiced this tradition since the pre-Islamic times. Originally, the rite of murwakala was performed as part of the opening scene of wayang purwa (Moerdowo 1982, pp. 13, 21, 22; Ulbricht 1970, pp. 14–21; Florida 1995, p. 273). Ancient mantras on the myth of world creation are narrated for all age audience. The message of the myth is made clear by certain characters in various power play scenes that the world necessitates liberation from perishability and care for its sustainability. In order to achieve this, collective awareness is evoked and mobilized as moral power.

Despite its nebulous and esoteric conceptualization, the importance of ruwatan for the Javanese world lies in the call for the collective care and the awareness of mortality that unity and harmony are not by chance, but the outcome from communal intention and effort. Scholars such as Foley (2001), Headly (2000, pp. 8–9, 91), and van Groenendael (1985) have observed the murwakala rite as part of ruwatan in many parts of Javanese places from West, Central, and East that the wayang performance led by dhalang gives the sense of dread and danger in terms of exoticism. The dhalang and their wayang performances are considered the strategic vehicle of Javanese culture that conveys, disseminates, and sustains ancient Javanese teaching with an entertainment package.

Even though the origin of the wayang purwa was probably from the Neolithic Ages the evolution of its content and performance concerning the rite of murwakala was inseparable from the assimilation of Indic civilization—such as Ramayana and Mahabharata—and indigenous Javanese culture. For the Javanese the wayang tradition is more than just entertainment. Rather, the puppet show is one of the sources of ethics and aesthetics as well as living philosophy.

Sociologically speaking, the wayang tradition in Java is also the voice of the ordinary and rural people. However, the effectiveness of wayang tradition in Javanese society lies in its artistically entertaining capacity that incorporates ancient messages and modern issues in a lively, attractive performance. Albeit the wayang performance as the medium and agent of cultural syncretism has been developing in various forms and styles, the main message of its presentation is clear that it invokes and articulates the Javanese world in the present context; this includes depicting the world with its ideals, dangers, constraints, and opportunities. The dhalang and its sponsor usually perform a specific theme and story of ancient legend, Ramayana or Mahabharata, that is actually in response to the current affairs in their societies, ranging from political, to economic, to social issues. Murwakala is one important theme that signifies the care for crises. The most important aspect of the rite of murwakala as well as ruwatan is to set the identity and boundary of the Javanese world within the historical and collective values of being Javanese, which is conveyed with the performance of shadow puppets.

Unlike other traditional cultures in the archipelago, the Javanese ancient tradition continues to live on today through the wayang performance. Since Islamic influence was in effect in Javanese societies, the role and function of the ancient priests (mpu, pandita) had been transformed and entrusted to the puppet master (dhalang). Such continuity is not the case in South Nias and Tana Toraja. The indigenous priests in both regions (ere and to mina) have been gradually dysfunctional and disappearing since the European missionaries established their Christian churches in their villages. In Java, indigenous traditions have been prevailing up until today through their performance arts such as wayang and gamelan that work as an artistically entertaining vehicle of the ancient world.

Artistic Principles and Artists in the Javanese Tradition

In Javanese culture, unity is achieved with the principle of identity between the creator and the creation that is expressed with the concept of manunggal; the unity itself is a central symbol of power (Anderson 2007, p. 36). The need for unity and harmony as well as sustainability lies in the fact that life (hurip) in the Javanese beliefs system is the synergy of dualistic characters and phenomena of nature. The reality of life and the world for the Javanese is unconceivable without the accord and wholeness of sky and earth, male and female, form and content, as well as outside and inside (Yumarma 1996). Accordingly, the task of a work of art is the act of bringing everything different together toward unity, harmony, and sustainability. The fear of disintegration and disarray urges the necessity for unity, harmony, and sustainability. In return, every being has to have their proper place with unique and particular function and contribution to the Javanese world. Accordingly, there is no concept of otherness but the unknown or beyond the boundary of the Javanese world and language.

Being an artist in the Javanese world is being in the proper position according to seniority, education, experience, maturity, and community service. The room for creative innovation and improvisation is to comply with the principle of harmony within the community. It implies that in the traditional way, individual freedom is likely out of question (Mulder 2005, p. 70).

Javanese animism believes that every being is a container (wadah) containing cosmic power (Jiwa or isi). Respect (ngajeni) to other beings is demonstrated with offering (sajen, sesajen), a bunch of flowers and foods at periodic times within five days of the Javanese calendar. Burning incense is part of the ritual for such offerings. Such a practice pertains to ancient and pre-Hindu rituals, as a sign of respect to the ancestors and the invisible power in the cosmos. They believe in the wholeness that human work is nothing unless it is imbued with the power of nature that flows into the form (lair) as its content (bathin).

Making things is conceived to work together with others, including the invisible. Being able to create and compose something from the earth is not to see humans as the master of being. The Javanese people believe that manmade things are indispensable from the transfer of energy from cosmos to the form. Consequently, the transfer needs to be handled with care and respect. This is not because of moral consideration. Naturally, it is about the awareness of being and remembering called eling. The state of eling is considered as the mental state of being able to listen to and to act according to respect (hormat, ngajeni) and dignity (praja, kaprajan). The Javanese people were trained from their childhood to realize that the way to be safe and healthy is not only to be loyal (mituhu) and sincere (jujur), but also to develop the sense of inner strength (mantep bathin). The training is exercised with fasting (pasa) and modest living (prihatin). All this is to improve the capacity for self-control of nourishment and want. Being able to know the difference between need and want is central for Javanese cultural training. In many cases, the outcome of this training is to lead people to be less assertive and not spontaneous.

Shying away from expressing their feelings and thoughts is a common phenomenon found among the Javanese people. Here, something good and beautiful in terms of human conduct is about subtlety. Foreigners get the impression that Javanese people are apathetic because they are too polite and sometimes misunderstand it. There is, of course, a way to invoke participation for Javanese people if they are asked personally.

Culturally, Javanese tradition has been developed with the concept of what being Javanese is; that is, ethically humble (andah asor), respective of others (ngajeni), and culturally cultivated (alus) (Sutton 1991, p. 21; Hughes-Freeland 2008, p. 82). Succinctly, being Javanese is being human in respect to other beings, either tangible or intangible. Thus, any determination concerning being in time is conceived as the way toward being somewhat ethically civilized based on polite comportment and respectful language. It is concerned with making something raw, coarse, and natural to be fine, subtle, and cultural.

The intensive training for self-control shapes and builds Javanese personality, which is, in many ways, more comfortable as listener and reader instead of speaker. Traditionally, seniority and experience play very important roles in communication. Often, seniority hinders and blocks to reveal something good and beautiful too. However, the Javanese people believe the reveal of the beautiful is unavoidable. It is only a matter of time. Being patient to accept (sabar nrima) is part of an inner strength exercise in Javanese tradition. Everything good and beautiful will come up out of the necessity for the world and community that is in terms of rahayuning jagad.

Making things within the system of the work of art is indispensable from the purpose of being Javanese. In other words, making things is to bring forth something from a naturally raw to a culturally cooked state of being. Conceptually, being Javanese is nothing but bringing forth resources from natural rawness to the world. Nature as the resource of the work of art is not subject to domination by human reason and action. The Javanese way of dealing with nature is to disclose its potency for being close to the hand of man based on sensibility (rasa).

Sensibility in the Javanese language is composed of the Sanskrit loan word rasa, literally meaning aesthetically sensitive and sensibly empathetic. Rasa in the Javanese sense is not simply the feeling of somewhat. Rather, it is about the mindfully aesthetic experience that is not only about sensibility but also about meaning (Geertz 1960, pp. 235–58; Benamou 1998). Under the notion of rasa, the act of carefully listening becomes the primordial stipulation for dealing with any phenomenon of being, whether insight of mind or in the actuality of the world. Being able to listen is somewhat decisive in the act of letting rasa be intact with what is coming to show itself. Under this notion, the relationship between people and the world is more to rely on intuition instead of reasonable doubt against otherness. In doing so, humankind and nature/resources are more within the immediate scenes of action, which are not associated within the stance of subject-object relationship. The relationship is, to a certain extent, based on interplay in the search for wholeness; it is through disclosure of each other by means of technique (makarya).

The artists in Javanese society live mostly from their relationship with their patron. Beyond the palace, the ruling class (priyayi) and merchants mostly dominate the traditional patronage of wayang performance. The patrons invite (nanggap) performance artists (wayang, troupes, dancers, singers, musicians, etc.) on a contractual basis. The rites of passage—such as circumcisions, weddings, and harvest thanksgivings—are the most common occasions for contract clientele. Inviting professional artists for family occasions and celebrations has been part of traditional clientele among the Javanese villagers until today. The classical description of the life and work of professional artists is probably to be found in Serat Centhini especially in describing the freelance artist cebolang (Anderson 2006, pp. 171–289; Santoso et al., 2006). Even though the writers of Serat Centhini were under the order and direction of Pakubuwana V that began in 1814, the Javanese world in the story is far away from the palace. The story covers rural and ordinary people in various places of Java with the absence of the Dutch from the scenes though the colonial rule that was strongly present during the period of the story, circa the beginning of the seventeenth century.

Since ancient times court artists and writers in Java have been part of the state bureaucrats. Their respectful title is mpu, meaning learned and wise person with specific literary or artistic skills. In the context of state/palace, the relationship between the artists and their clients, especially the king, is indispensably about the patronage of power. All works are mostly part of the ruling reign’s interest and point of view. Prior to King Airlangga’s reign, there are barely any names of artists or writers who worked on the surviving architecture, murals, poetry (kakawin), gamelan compositions, dances, stories (babad), and literature (susastera) of the period between the eighth and the eleventh century. Airlangga became the first king of Kahuripan in 1019 and began to establish his power in East Java by making peace with Sriwijaya. Under his reign (1019 AD–1049 AD), Airlangga consolidated his power with strong leadership and religious tolerance.

Physical and epigraphic evidence in the area around Kediri confirmed the literary activities during the Airlangga rein. Beside the Javanese versions of classical Ramayana and Mahabharata, Airlangga’s tenure might be that for the first time the original works of literature were written in the Old Javanese language by the Javanese writer Mpu Kanwa. Kanwa wrote Arjuna Wiwaha depicting the marriage of Arjuna who is to adore Airlangga as the avatar of Wisnu on earth. The relationship between artists/writers and the ruler under Airlangga’s kingship is clearly for the pride of power and its state ideology. The patronage continued in Singasari and Majapahit, especially during Hayam Wuruk’s reign (1350 AD–1389 AD).

The considerable breakup of the traditional patronage never comes into being either in pre-colonial or in the colonial Java. However, a slightly radical movement of artistic self-expression did happen in Surakarta Palace in the middle of the eighteenth century when the poet Raden Ngabehi Ranggawarsita worked under the reign of Pakubuwono VII. As the king at the time, Pakubuwana VII confirmed him as court poet (pujangga kraton). Since the king did not pay too much attention to literature, the state gave Ranggawarsita more space, not only for his creative writings, but also for his talent in envisioning the Javanese world. In the past, such similar prophetic narration had been delivered by King Jayabaya (1135–1157) of Menang Kediri East Java. In many ways, the poems by Ranggawarsita are unconventional for Javanese court tradition. Unlike his grandfather and predecessor Yasadipura II, he speaks his own mind in his poems in a prophetic way. He continued to work as court poet under Pakubuwana VIII and Pakubuwana IX until his mysterious demise in 1873. The cause of his death remains unclear today.

What Is Art in Javanese Culture?

Even though in Javanese culture there is no word for what art is, the Javanese people understand its sense and purpose. Any endeavor for the Javanese people is to achieve its quality in terms of karya. Making a work of art is generally understood under the notion of makarya. Karya is a Sanskrit loan word that has been adopted by Javanese culture to mean the work with its usefulness and contribution to the well-being of the world and the community. As a matter of fact, karya is an artistically well-thought-out work that brings forth the world with specific value and meaning. Karya in the broadest sense is all human works, which are functionally and aesthetically well considered in bringing forth culture, in terms of refined things and behaviors. The opposite of culture is urakan, meaning coarse, rude, and impolite.

Albeit it is unfashionable in Javanese tradition to deal with the concept of art, it does not mean that artistic work does not have any specific signification. Concepts related to the work of art and aesthetic experience in Javanese culture are wide-ranging in meanings and expressions. In such a broad spectrum, there are at least three main categories of works of art in Javanese culture: susastra (literature), karawitan (music), and karya (general works of art).

The characteristic of karya should contain the integrity of ethics and aesthetics that shows and incorporates the idea of well-mannered, refined, and restrained (alus) (Hughes-Freeland 2008, p. 82). The sense of alus is applicable for matters, words, and conduct. Accordingly, work and deed are purposive as far as toward its refinement and aesthetically well-treated, according to the Javanese system of values. Refinement in this sense is simply not about fine expression, articulation, and interaction. Rather, Javanese refinement is about proper position, orientation, form, style, and fashion.

In many ways, the appropriateness of alus requires ubiquitous acceptance, subtlety, and delicacy. All this has been evolving for centuries with its root in agriculture-based feudalism. Culture in this sense is more than just a set of shared meanings associated with customary practices (Berger 1995, p. 32). Rather, the Javanese understand culture in the context of budhi. The Sankrit loan word budhi in Javanese context has been evolved and conceived as the powerful source and system of mind and action; based on that one is able to create, establish, develop, conserve, and sustain ideas, feelings, spirits, memories, ingenuities, hopes, and ideals. In daily life, the idea of culture as budhi is practiced as aesthetic and moral standard. Language and manners convey the budhi as a standard of appropriation. Nevertheless, budhi is nothing and useless unless it is imbued with forces, drives, and passions of life known as daya. The synergy of budhi and daya is incorporated with the concept of budhaya, meaning literally culture.

Then, making something beautiful, useful, and insightful as well as well-mannered behavior is inseparable from the essence of Javanese culture (budhi). Beyond and above all appearances, the Javanese people understand the work of art in various forms, ways, and fashions as part of their living philosophy that humankind as a society has to cultivate itself artistically and socially. All this learning process is conceived by the Javanese people in the concept of nglakoni, meaning walking along. Accordingly, living is walking as well as experiencing the now in an infinite way. This chapter is an attempt to unfold conceptual foundations of aesthetic experience in Javanese culture.

To have a brief idea of the Javanese world, there is no appropriate single word to describe their culture and tradition better than the notion of syncretism. In the Javanese language, syncretic phenomena are contextually associated with the deeply emotive concept and word manunggal, meaning to become one (Anderson 2007, pp. 23–24). Unity (utuh) and harmony (rukun) become the most obsessed concepts in Javanese tradition. The phenomena of syncretism are revealed in various activities and products. The Javanese people understand the concept in terms of domestication. It is the way the Javanese people deal with making, living, and working. Syncretism in the Javanese language is conceived in the concept of nglaras or laras (Yumarma 1996, pp. 140–45). The concept is central in Javanese thought that being everything antagonistic, dualistic, and dialectic into oneness.

As an integral part of culture, the Javanese art and aesthetic is indispensable from the act of molding myriad forms, ideas, and fashions into a newly established system. The purpose of the unity is to be Javanese (jawa), meaning to be human, culturally cultivated, and mature (Weiss 2006). Being Javanese is being human with good manners and strong self-control. All this is composed of the concept of alus, literally meaning refined and mature, the opposite of kasar or rude and immature. The ultimate goal of nglaras is to achieve the refinement, alus, of work and behavior.

The purpose of the work of art in Javanese tradition is indivisible from the Javanese struggle from the world in the darkness (kalatida) to that in the light (kalasuba). It is not about making something for the sake of itself in terms of artistic self-expression. And it is not about individual freedom either. The work concerning aesthetic experience for Javanese people is about well-being (sugeng slamet) of the world and the community. Their works are demonstrated in architecture (wayang kulit), shadow puppet theater, jogged (dance), susastra (literature), ketoprak (folk theater), gamelan orchestra, batik, and kris (dragger).

Although today Javanese kingdoms still exist in Yogyakarta and Surakarta, their roles and functions are more spiritually symbolic rather than politically involved. In the field of artwork, court arts and folk arts live together in different purpose. Court arts serve their works for court ceremonies and rituals (Jessup 1990, pp. 73–102). They belong to preserve and sustain cultural values in terms of court pride and heritage. Indeed, glorifying the past for spiritual identity and unity of Javanese world becomes the central purpose of the court arts. This includes performing annual and occasional celebrations that emulate and simulate as well as signify the Javanese world.

In contrast to court arts, folk arts live and sustain the living philosophy of Javanese culture in a blunt way (ngoko). Folk arts in Javanese daily life are understood under the notion of tontonan kawula, meaning performance for commoners. Beyond its culturally pleasing capacity, folk arts play very important roles in preserving, developing, and sustaining Javanese culture and tradition. Unlike court arts, folk arts give more room for innovations and improvisations without having to follow traditional patterns and canons religiously.

The process of enculturation in the Javanese sense is understood under the notion of laras, nglaras. Accordingly, bringing forth different elements from various sources into synthesis of form, expression, and articulation belongs to the disposition of the Javanese way of making things. The sense of nglaras is not simply about compromise. Nglaras in the broadest sense is to work out everything foreign in compliance with Javanese values and customs. It is properly understood as the way of inner and mental exercise (tirakat) to accept something new and strange and to work it out internally. The aim of this inner exercise is to encounter the antagonistic elements and aspects that enable them to be fit with Javanese culture. Frankly speaking, it is the way Javanese people domesticate and tame (nglulutake) and foreign elements coming into their realm of culture.

Arts, Beliefs, and Values

Although most thoughtful concepts concerning Javanese tradition are originated from Sanskrit culture, locally customized contextuality has been made for being properly synchronized with what has been already available in indigenous concepts. Literacy and script have been adopted from India under the influence of Hindu and Buddhism at the beginning of the first century. Since then, Sanskrit culture has been adopted by Javanese tradition within the framework of syncretism with indigenous pantheistic animism.

Traditionally, Javanese culture puts the principle of ethics for respect to any being—living or natural elements—within the system of beings as a whole. In the Javanese sense this wholeness (utuh manunggal) is conceived as the purpose of the Javanese world. Being obsessed with this purpose often leads to intolerance for differences; in certain ways it leads toward a prophetic mission for colonializing others on behalf of the wholeness of the Javanese world.

Traditionally, arts, values, and meanings in Javanese society find their common ground on the subject of symbolism. Despite the dualistic patrimonial social structure of the lords (priyayi) and the commoners or ordinary people (kawula, wong cilik), the Javanese society maintains and regards the subtlety of conducts and works as part of their aesthetic worthiness. Molding myriad forms, things, ideas, and fashions into a coherent whole is culturally a Javanese way of making things. This act of molding is understood by Javanese people under the concept of manunggal, which is derived from the word tunggal, meaning single, one, and entity. It is probably not an exaggeration to say that Javanese culture is always striving to syncretically integrate everything with the Javanese being. Based on this most likely cultural obsession, Javanese imperialism, tolerance, and pretentiousness come to be understandable.

Bringing everything coming from outside Javanese cultural being is Javanese cultural strategy in dealing with otherness. The strategy of manunggal is well known among Javanese people as nglaras. Indeed, subtlety and decency play important roles in the act of nglaras. Thus, nglaras is experiencing the being of something with intentional intercourse for establishing and developing integration into the Javanese world.

The influence of Javanese culture in the Indonesian world is in many respects dominant because of its underlying principle of making up with others to establish and to develop newly emerging things within the sensibly learning system of nglaras. Accordingly, it is not simply to avoid confrontations or to deny the contradictions. Rather, nglaras is a flexible approach to work out any differences based on the strength of silence for harmony (cocok, rukun) and oneness (tunggal, manunggal). The Javanese people value harmony and unity as their way of life (Suseno 1997).

The Javanese confidence in harmony and in oneness of the world has been establishing the sense of purpose of the works of art in myriad forms, matters, and fashions. Hence, making in the context of art is makarya, that is, to bring forth beings into harmony and wholeness. Only on this condition do the Javanese people understand the beautiful as apik (good), slamet (safe), asri (naturally delighted), rahayu (well-being), and lestari (sustainable).

To come into harmony and oneness, Javanese people have to learn to control themselves to behave properly according to Javanese ethics (karma) (Berman 1998, p. 17). For Javanese people, harmony (rukun) and oneness (utuh manunggal) are indispensable from the world they understand. Harmony is conceived as being comfortable and in compliance with others not only based on respect and mutual understanding, but also based on fitting in hierarchical ordering system of beings.

In social life as well as in artistic composition, being harmonious is being able to put oneself on proper seat in the whole system. Any being in this cultural system has its own place with a well-established structure that works in the service of oneness. Subsequently, subsuming of egoistic interest (pamrih) to the community of beings in which the harmonious relation (rukun) of members to each other is its indwelling causes. Finding the middle way or compromise is the best option for the Javanese way of life, instead of competition of ideas or records in a sportive way. Embracing rivals for compromise is conceived as a peaceful way to sustain the Javanese world.

Any work of art in its essential way is meant to achieve the state of being in which the inspiring silence is. Indeed, deep contemplation (samadi) is necessarily done (Atkinson and Errington 1990, p. 54). Even though in many cases mysticism comes into play to imbue the importance of contemplation, the sense of samadi lies in the readiness for listening. Nevertheless, it does not mean that meditation is simply a concentration in the search for form and expression. Rather, the most important thing for achieving the inspiring silence through meditation is to pre-empt judgment and to be open and ready to listen to what is going to come to mind.

Liberating the self from any interest, pretension, and prejudice is the way of making within the concept of makarya. The Javanese proverb says sepi ing pamrih rame ing gawe, meaning free yourself from any interest and keep doing the work. Making the work of art in this way is to get into the work and within the work in the search for wholeness. Bringing forth every element together is to let its being be revealed according to its own properties and qualities. Making in terms of makarya is not something apart from recognizing and bringing forth its nature to be close with humanity through his or her hand. It is the reason why work in the Javanese tradition is allusively articulated with the word anasta, literally meaning hand. Is it not because of the human hand that the world is established and developed?

What is actually the purpose of the work of art in Javanese culture? As already mentioned, harmony and oneness are inseparable from the purpose of the Javanese world, that is, to beautify the being of beings as a whole. Beautifying the world (memayu ayuning bhawana) is essentially the purpose of all human work on earth. Accordingly, subsuming any individual want and need is the precondition for being able to dedicate all effort and endeavor to get into the law and principle of integration into wholeness. The integration is demonstrated with the quality of synthesis without losing traces of its authentic origin. The work as the manmade thing is conceived as a new being with its own power, which is in Javanese tradition called greget (Wibisono and Kurniasih 2002, p. 264).

Any successful work of art is believed to have a spiritual power of greget (Suharto 1998) that invokes intense human feelings and sensibility for empathy as well as for getting along with the audience. Under the notion of greget, the work of art is able to bring forth human experience with the sense of harmony within the whole composition. Correspondingly, any part in the system of work demonstrates its significant role and contribution to the whole story within the work.

Perfection (utuh), stability (jejeg), and free from vested interest (sepi ing pamrih) belong to what Javanese culture considers aesthetically pleasing. In Javanese culture, perfection (utuh, sampurna) of the work is understood if the work is open-ended, in which the beginning and the end come into terms with an infinite cycle that grows from micro to macro cosmos. The ground of this perfection is based on the beliefs that every being is identical and contains the power of being. Without this identity, beings are not able to stand together as a whole.

Stability (jejeg) is another condition of the work of art for being able to take part in the whole composition or system. Standing in line within natural laws is one important principle to achieve stability in composition. The notion of stability is also to address the work with essential quality that is not untruthful or hiding something. The concept of jejeg in the broadest sense of the word is applicable for somewhat persistent upholding adoration (sajen) and respectfulness (kajen). The work of art without stability is not respectful in the sense of characterlessness because it does not contain any constructive element and structure for being whole.

Moreover, attaining what beauty is is necessarily free from any interest (pamrih) (Mulder 2005, p. 18). Interest never stands without the will to control, to hold, and to take into possession. Interest in the sense of pamrih is a selfish pursuit for one’s own advantage associated with disrespect to the whole welfare. In the work of art, interest endangers the stability of the Javanese world, which is established and sustained within the traditional structure of communal identity. Any individualistic profile and propensity that challenges traditional values may be considerably jeopardizing the collectively established community as well as oneness.

Creative and innovative efforts of artists under the Javanese tradition are necessarily subtle and delicate in unveiling improvement and discovery by persistently working within the framework of respect to tradition. What is unfavorable for Javanese is not about the creativeness, but the excess of this creative capacity to the concept of self that may lead to the state of being overconfident as well as arrogant and ignorant. Nevertheless, the Javanese world has learned much from modernity to accommodate individual artistic endeavors with respect to local resources in the search for natural harmony and oneness through nglaras.

Javanese Concepts of Aesthetics

In a general way, the Javanese world for art is harmonious and whole in a good shape (apik) as long as there is no internal conflict within the work that leads us into confusion of meaning. Good (apik) is understood as somewhat complete and well-organized. The aesthetic concept of apik is to emphasize the necessity for order within the work that provides the elements with role and contribution to the whole.

Moreover, the sense of apik is to invoke the feeling of an all right and favorable state of being. Within the sense of apik every element in the system is related to each other according to certain structures of role and contribution so that one for all and vice versa comes into being. In addition, apik is actually applicable for the logically interrelated system that enables the work to be qualified as the thing with a certain designation of meaning. Succinctly, apik is the underlying concept for something in orderly organization and perfect being.

Only human being, not the work of art for the Javanese people is qualified to be beautiful if its effect on human being is healthy and delightful under the notion of rahayu or in concise ayu. The pleasure of rahayu lies in the capacity of the work of art to bring about a healthy sphere and influence to human being. Rahayu is an aesthetic concept for describing the quality of feminine being with elegant movement, well-proportioned posture and form, and compassionately emanating aura. In many cases, ayu or rahayu is also applicable to the work of art representing feminine being.

As mentioned earlier, the sense of what beauty is for the Javanese people is not somewhat pleasurable in terms of self-indulgence. Rather, the sense of beauty is essentially experienced as the sense of being in wholeness in which self and others come into terms with spiritual identity. Accordingly, time collapses into infinity of spiritually inspiring silence (hening). The beautiful and peaceful are never divisible for Javanese aesthetics in terms of rahayu. It is neither about pretty or attractive things, nor about sensually and visually appealing things. The sense of rahayu as aesthetic experience is about being intact with the world as presence. It is the experience of being uplifting in tranquility.

The opposite concept to rahayu is ganteng, describing the handsome and gentlemanly character for male being. However, ganteng is strangely applied for the work of art. The other concept related to male character for the work of art is gagah, meaning gallant, strong, and sturdy. Gender categories of aesthetic character are mostly applicable for human being. Other living beings are usually not qualified to fall into the male or female category for describing their gender character.

Nevertheless, in the performing arts, such as wyang wong, feminine and masculine characters play an important role in revealing human character. Bridging the antagonistic category between male and female characters is, however, to be unveiled in the characters of Srikandi and Puntadewa in the epics of the Javanese versions of Mahabharata as well as Sarpakanaka and Laksmana in the epics of the Javanese version of Ramayana. Srikandi and Sarpakanaka are known as tough women with masculine traits, whereas Puntadewa and Laksmana are male with feminine characteristics. In sculpture, the embodiment of male-female in single form was found in the statue of Ardhanari from the Kediri area of East Java circa the twelfth century. The statue shows the hybrid figures of Wisnu and Parvati.

Furthermore, the corresponding concept of rahayu for describing a beautiful event is endah. The concept of endah is pertinent for somewhat beautiful in respect to its effect on human feelings of pleasure and comfort. Something is endah in the way of bringing about delight into our sensibility based on aura and sphere of happening. Unfolding the natural character of landscape and its pictorial composition is expressed with the concept of endah in which the sense of delight comes into being from experiencing its spectacle. However, a beautiful picture of a landscape is not appropriate to be qualified with the concept of endah. Rather, such beauty is described with the concept of apik.

The sense of endah lies in its immediately experienced beauty of something within its natural setting. Fitting with its context or setting is subject to be qualified with the concept of endah, provided that the thing is not only suitably proper in its scenery or background, but also if it brings forth the composition as a whole with aesthetically well-considered property. Properly fitting into surroundings in Javanese culture is addressed with the concept of cocok, literally meaning appropriate, correct, and matched with each other. Thus, on the grounds of cocok, something that fits in its context and setting is qualified to achieve the beautiful character of endah.

What is the concept of beauty in music for Javanese people? As mentioned earlier, in Javanese culture, pleasure in the context of self-indulgence is considered immoral or an unethically proper experience. Beautiful music here is not somewhat pleasurable for being experienced on an individual basis. Rather, the music of gamelan orchestra is not performed as the main menu of Javanese entertainment, but as background, or back sound or supporting system for shadow puppet plays, dances, and ritual events. Of course, harmonious rhythm of pentatonic (slendro) as well heptatonic-pentatonic (pelog) gamelan music is highly regarded to enhance and to support the main performance with appropriate rhythmic pace, softness, and loudness, as well as depth and width.

All over again, the aesthetic concept of apik is applicable for describing gamelan music with perfect quality of arrangement, performance, and composition. Music appreciation in the Javanese tradition is composed of the concept of nglaras, meaning to get along with the rhythmic pace of human walking. Going along with the rhythm of walking (mlaku) is essentially the Javanese way of dealing with time. Nglaras is nothing but putting the temporal structure of being in alignment with walking.

Being as well as dwelling in Javanese culture is conceivably composed of the concept of walking (mlaku). Accordingly, temporality of being is signified rhythmically as naturally as possible. The purpose of the work of human being including art is to integrate into the rhythmic universe as experienced with the pace of walking in the sense of laku, lakon, nglakoni, meaning experiencing for being within the rhythm of heartbeat. The Javanese people experience this as the way to be in touch with the now immediately.

Indeed, ups and downs as well as fast and slow belong to the variations of human walk through time. However, coming back to find the rhythm of walking is the Javanese way of being in the world within the concept of nglaras. Being as well as dwelling (lungguh, lenggah) is actually sitting to experience the rhythm of heartbeat while listening to what is coming. The task of being as well as living is not to achieve something under the need of self-actualization. Rather, for the Javanese people, the sense of being as well as living is able to bring forth what is coming and what is given within the framework of integration into the rhythm of heartbeat as experienced by walking through time. Where is the end of the Javanese journey? This question sounds trivial for Javanese culture because by being able to find the rhythm of life we are already within the end of what being as well as dwelling is. This is according the Javanese culture the essence of what beauty is. And the purpose of being is nothing but gratifying and beatifying the world (memayu ayuning bhawana) based on sensibility.

In Javanese culture, being an artist is being part of the community with the Javanese aesthetic goal of being collective artwork. Artwork needs selfless dedication and devotion. It is not for glorifying the artist because the artist is simply the worker like any other laborers in the community. And the artwork should be a celebration of community. Even though the works of art in Javanese culture include many fields of literature, visual, and performance arts, wayang, batik, and gamelan are most the popular and representative works today.

Wayang

What is the aesthetics of wayang? Wayang is more than just a theater for Javanese culture and tradition. It is all about the philosophy of alive and contemporary political and social opinions. Even though the story of wayang performance is mostly depicted from the Indian epics of Ramayana and Mahabharata, the essential line of drama is about Javanese thought and ethics in a communicative way. The role and function of dhalang is a great communicator of Javanese ideals. Interestingly, sociopolitical affairs belong to the subject matter of the wayang performance. The occasion for dealing with the play represents the Javanese world in contemporary circumstances. The play is considered the shadow of the reality of the Javanese world. The word dhalang literally means the director, orator, and actor in a single role. The art of performing wayang is the art of making the Javanese world alive in the play. The resume of the play is obviously the prevailing of culture against rawness, greed, injustice, deception, complacency, and arrogance.

Concerning aesthetics, dhalang plays a significant role in the aesthetic quality of performance. This includes his/her knowledge and skills on dramatic storytelling and current sociopolitical affairs. As a matter of fact, a good dhalang is a well-rounded artist with a critical sensibility for his/her contemporary world. His/her biting remarks, intelligent commentaries, and clever jokes on current affairs are needed in the midnight scenes when the punakawan (representative figures of commoner) come to the stage. In doing so, he/she does not make the audience bored or fall asleep from the beginning, at 9:00–10:00 p.m., to the end, at 4:00–5:00 a.m.

At the opening scenes, an aesthetically well-considered wayang performance performs mostly and commonly a series of scenes depicting the ideal Javanese character in the form of satria. The ordinary world is commonly presented as the realm of chaos, disorder, and confusion as the consequence of greed, desire, and anger. The main character, a satria, is called to be in charge in response to the occasion. The story will be followed with the scenes depicting a series of ordeals of the satria: he/she has to prepare his/her knowledge (elmu), skills (kanugaran), and personal maturity (mantep satria) to enable him/her to restore the unity, harmony, and sustainability of the world.

The origin of wayang is from the pre–Hindu-Buddhist times. Originally, wayang was performed with wood-carved puppets. The modern wayang is performed with leather puppets or human performers. Wayang in Javanese society is an educationally entertaining performance for all ages. What is important to deliver in wayang performance is to sustain Javanese cultural values generation to generation. The all-night performance is directed and performed by a dhalang who plays the shadow puppets, music, and voices of all characters.

In terms of aesthetics, wayang is a demonstration of experience about empathy (welas), shame (isin), compassion (asih), joy (seneng), respect (hormat), and hesitation (sungkan). The fight between culture and natural rawness is represented by the satrias and the raksasa. Tension between antagonistic elements and aspects becomes the most interesting part of aesthetic experience in wayang performance. Nonetheless, the drama of Javanese ideals comes into play with various tests and ordeals before the main character is able to restore and establish the world in order. The most important thing of wayang performance is for its audience to align itself (nglaras) to the contemporary world with full awareness of ancient wisdom and teaching. The purpose of nglaras is to become a satria budhi luhung (a well-mannered person with cultivated characters).

Batik

Batik represents Javanese graphic design as well as painting. The art of batik is emphasized on the pattern dated back to pre–Hindu-Buddhist influence. Mbatik in the Javanese language means engraving and painting in details. Geometrical patterns with double spiral motifs are dominant. Its origin is presumably from the Dong-son metal work culture (Bulbeck 2004, pp. 428–39). Repetitive form and an earth color scheme on white cotton dominate the design of batik. However, black, brown, and dark yellow are foremost in most classic batik. The most important thing for classic batik design is to harmonize (nglaras) the pattern and color in a gentle and delicate manner. Indeed, the aesthetic questions of batik are simple, concerning its inspiring capacity with motifs and patterns, its rapturous energy of uniqueness, and its provocative abstraction of figures (McCabe-Elliot and Brake 2004, p. 198). Avoiding something bizarre and blunt in the composition is obvious. The message of batik is to celebrate the world in the background. Subtlety of festivity belongs to the purpose of what batik design is all about.

Indeed, there are versions of batik design for occasional celebration and daily use. The daily clothes of ordinary people are lurik. The common people wear lurik instead of classic batik. Technically speaking lurik is ikat textile. The difference between batik and lurik is not only its pattern, but also its production method and process. By design, lurik is simple and less elaborate in pattern and color. This is understandable because of its weaving technique of single or double ikat. Most lurik have only simple line pattern and a dark indigo color. All this is to incorporate the notion of modesty (sa’ madya) in the life of common people. Wearing lurik is to identify oneself as commoner. In many cases, only priyayi (the ruling class) people wear batik. Even though it is not obvious in their appearances, subtlety in social status in Javanese culture and tradition is shown in the clothes they wear. The sense of nglaras is demonstrated with the way people wear in casual and formal occasions in which earth color and similar pattern come into play.

Gamelan

The other important work of art in Javanese tradition is gamelan music. The important thing for gamelan music is its interlocking tonal and rhythmic pattern. Extensive historical study on gamelan by Sumarsam confirms that this Javanese musical tradition has survived through various ages—from Hindu and Islam to colonial Dutch and modernity—because foreign elements and ideas are blended and imposed together in a harmonious way (Sumarsam 1995). As a matter of fact, such syncretism is and was not always smooth. Tensions between Hindu-Buddhist and Islamic elements were evident as depicted in Serat Cebolek. However, in terms of nglaras conflict resolutions have been subject to be worked out with intelligible adjustment and justification. The conflicts of ideas between Javanese gamelan and Islamic tradition has been subtly unfolded and explored by Javanese artists and writers in Serat Centhini, in which playing gamelan music and reciting Koran/Qur’an (ngaji dhikir) are thoughtfully identified, similarly compared, and reasonably justified (Patel 2008, pp. 325–56; Sutton 1991, p. 236).

Listening and playing to gamelan music is inseparable from the way of life in terms of walking (mlaku) and in terms of nglaras. Both activities are experienced as spiritually entertaining moments for recollection, awareness, and remembering (eling). Even though the music of gamelan is featured mainly with metallophones, xylophones, and gongs, the leading instrument is the drum (kendang), followed by bamboo flutes, bowed and plucked strings, and vocalists (sinden). Gamelan as traditional orchestra is mostly performed for special events of memorial celebration. The main function of gamelan music in Javanese culture is to nurture laras in terms of the unity and harmony of mind (jiwa), body (raga), and spirit (sukma), with cyclic patterns of predictable coincidences. The sense of living as walking and experiencing “the now” is performed with gamelan music. People sit on the floor by folding their legs while playing and listening to gamelan music. Traditionally, the performers and their listeners are together, relaxed but mindful. This is what nglaras in action is.

Concluding Remarks

Traditionally in Java, philosophy and artworks are indispensable from daily life. The works of art here are neither exclusive nor self-actualized. Rather, Javanese tradition and culture uphold artwork as collective works that preserve, develop, and sustain their traditional values on modesty, humility, and subtlety. Working on artistic things is inseparable from living in bringing forth everything toward harmony. This is the sense of what nglaras is all about. In daily life, the sense of nglaras is metaphorically experienced as walking. Walking (mlaku) is nothing but experiencing the now in infinite way on its own rhythm. It is the way to engage with the world based on the belief that being is sustained by gratitude (nrima) and compassion (asih). The purpose of artwork is nothing but to celebrate and contribute to the beautiful and peaceful world in terms of memayu ayuning bhawana.

Moreover, the sense of nglaras is appreciation of the rhythm of life and alignment with the temporal environment. The core of activity for this artistic appreciation is actually to exercise the mode of thinking in receptive mode. In doing so, the Javanese people are trained to accept what it is without emotional and thoughtful resistance. The presence of the works in various forms, styles, manners, and fashions is not to take under control, but to let them show themselves as they are in their own characteristics and properties. The Javanese concept for this acceptance is ngematke, literally meaning observing, inspecting, listening, reading, and appreciating.

Chapter Two

Pra Kuta Negara

Pre-Islamic States and Formative Urbanism in Java Indonesia

Even though most states and settlement centers had disappeared between the first and twelfth centuries, the traces of Indic influence were apparent. Their barely recognized traces in the past were found in scattered sources; from relics of monuments (candis), inscriptions (prasasti), poems (kakawin), and chronicles (babad), to myths (dongeng). Nonetheless, on urban history there is seemingly nothing left behind in terms of comprehensive archaeological evidence. Were pre-Islamic states without cities possible or did they have a different concept of urbanism? Hitherto, judging on urban settlements in the Indonesian context lacks the local knowledge and context of what urban is. The primary sources of this historical investigation are local concepts, artefacts, relics, and bibliographical history related to human settlement in Java Indonesia prior to the twelfth century.

Pre-Islamic Java

Studies on early urbanism in pre-Islamic Java have been presented by numerous scholars (Coedes 1944/1968; Wisseman-Christie 1986; Wisseman-Christie 1983; Coedes 1966; Ali 2011; Kulke 2001). Their contribution to our knowledge on ancient Javanese settlements is unquestionably significant. However, local ancient concepts concerning urban settlement still need further examining and explicating. This chapter argues that local concepts are potentially resourceful for historical analysis because ancient concepts and things were contextually related to and associated with each other in constructing the ancient world. What is the relationship between the concepts of state and urbanism in ancient Java? The question leads this chapter to explore aspects and ancient concepts related to early states and societies that are theoretically constituent for ideas or traces of urbanism. Hitherto, less attention has been paid to developing theoretical positions that enable us to understand the relationship between negara and urbanity. This fact is probably rooted in the deficiency of our understanding of the political culture that was shaped and had been shaped with negara as the generative concept of Indonesian towns and cities.

Hitherto, misconceptions have been inevitable because urbanism is narrowly understood as the morphological phenomenon of settlement with the concentration of populations. Rather, urbanism is essentially about the experience of politically organized settlements with a sustainable capacity for cultural cultivations and economic growth. Accordingly, development and growth are possible because there are available resources and the political stability of their production system, politically and environmentally. Besides, urbanism enables citizens to sustain their wealth, power, and capacity for something worthy as a nation or system of polity. Concentration of settlement is probably not a sole determinant variable for urbanism but apparently the outcome of mode, demand, and consequence of a cultural production system.

To understand the history of urbanism in Indonesia as a cultural phenomenon it is likely necessary to include the discourse on negara as a political concept. The reason is because the origin of urban settlement is inescapably the product of political culture and of interactions with others. It is political culture that makes towns and cities unique and contextual. Since urban settlement is the site of the concentration of power, unfolding and dismantling the concepts of hegemony and authority is crucial for a historical discourse on what urban is. Methodologically, this chapter is a hermeneutical investigation. It deals with the concepts of structure and the aspects of early urbanism in Java Indonesia. The focus of the chapter is to divulge and unfold local concepts concerning domains and community aspects that have been constructive to the world of the past and present. In order to find their original meanings of locality, dismantling and divulging the local and original concepts of place becomes the main task of the chapter. In doing so, urban history is not simply a descriptive narrative of the past but also a discursive interpretation of the precedent world and its political culture. Then the history of urbanism will be depicted within this socioculturally constructed reality in which negara as a concept is central and indispensable for urbanism in Indonesia.

Desa and Early Negara

In the history of Indonesian settlements, villages (wanua, kampong, gampong, banua, kabisu) are distinguished from towns (Javanese kuta or Malay kota) by their institutions and societies. Wanua is an autonomous, self-sufficient, and paternalistic settlement organization. Socially, ancient villages (wanuas) were constituted of dualistic antagonistic groups: noble class and commoners (Kroef 1954, pp. 842–62). Even though several villages (wanuas) stood independently (Bellwood et al. 1995, p. 195), a negara had to have several desa (villages), which surrounded and supported its political center (kuta negara). Historically, there was never a negara without desas. Negara and desa had been in coexistence since the adoption of Hindu and Buddhist influences from circa the fifth century. Desas and negara were not simply economically interdependent, in terms of a master/slave relationship, but they both also metaphysically supported each other, in terms of a parent-children relationship.

Undeniably, most indigenous rulers in Java embraced Hinduism and Buddhism between the fourth and tenth century, which enabled them to found a state negara. Prior to this possibility, they had to unite at least four villages surrounding their own wanua under their control. This was what in Javanese tradition was known as mancapat (Koentjaraningrat 1985, p. 100). However, it does not mean to consolidate the whole villages into a location. What is important for the mancapat concept is to build a chain of villages. The more powerful a state is, the longer the chain they build. Then the surrounding villages (tpi siring) were brought under their political control and granted with desa polity status. Despite the consolidation of villages under a single roof, authority was indigenous idea and practice, the transformation from chiefdom to state had been philosophically owing to Indic state influence. They did not only learn from the Indic civilization Hinduism and Buddhism, but also statecraft, literacy, and the way to control territory with a taxation and tribute system.

During the eighth century, there was a significant change in the development of Javanese settlements. The change is documented in the inscriptions of Dinaya, which mentions the Sanjaya dynasty from circa 732 AD (Coedes 1944/1968, p. 90). The development was denoted with the text indicating the replacement of concept from the indigenous settlement institution of wanua by negara or desa. As found repeatedly in several inscriptions in the region of Sleman Yogyakarta, desa was not understood by the Javanese culture in its original Sanskrit account for place and territory. Rather, in the ancient Javanese context, desa was a village settlement and community with a politically organized system of dualistic social groups: karaman consisting of elders (rama) and bhogi consisting of raka or rakryan (big or older brother) at the village ruling position; karaman is gentry of the village. Later on the raka or rakryan became the title of pre-Islamic Javanese aristocrats or noblemen. The title had been transformed from rakryan to raden since the Islamic Demak Sultanate with its first ruler Raden Patah.

Nonetheless, the integration of former wanuas into a negara meant that the village egalitarian way of life was gradually dissolved by a bureaucratic system of feudal polity. Evidently, it was impossible without a concentration and consolidation of power within the aristocrat group. The dissolution was also the cause of the weakening egalitarian assembly of the elders (karaman), meanwhile it was also the formative time for the more sophisticated agrarian landlord system in the Javanese society.

As a matter of fact, the adoption of Indic statecraft was subsequently not only a transformation of the title of wanua chieftain from raka or rakryan to dhatu or ratu, but also the shift of the role and function of leadership from the chief of community to the political leader of society. This implies a development of social status and stratification and more diversity in occupations and hierarchical chain of command. This early formative phase of state could have also been followed by the emergence of a hierarchically centralized system of negara.

The inscriptions in Central and East Java (Lokapala, Mantyasih, Wanua Tengah, and Sugih Manek) from the late ninth century further brought up the appearance of freeholds of settlement beyond desa called wateg, which was politically at the same level as the status of desa. The status of such a freehold had been traditionally granted by a ruler as political tactics to embrace other landlords without violent conquest. With such a good will, a ruler was in a position to gain loyalty and diminish potential rivals. High- and low-rank royal officers (samgat, pamgat, and mangilala drawya haji) and some chieftains enjoyed a tax exemption as well. The status of freehold could have been used to embrace potentially competing rulers for cooperation. In return, paying homage periodically to the seat of rulers, such as sowan dalem for sima ceremonies, must have been advised as a sign of veneration and loyalty (Tarling 1999, p. 214).

The sima in ancient Javanese tradition was the place with special status, free of taxation. It could be also the holy place of the king or ancestral heroes. Granting a sima status of land was a political move of the ruler to control his/her subordinate landowners. This required a state ritual and ceremony that confirmed and formalized the arrangement. It was also spiritually considered the birth and renewal of the state as the world. In doing so, the presence of the ruler on the occasion was intended to justify his/her status as the god on earth.

Moreover, there were some concepts related to settlement from the inscriptions during the era of Hindu-Buddhist states, such as wanua and negara, duhwan/kaduhwan, desa, dapur, thani, dalem, and thani. All these concepts had specific and unique meanings that covered the transformation processes of political culture prior to the time before the displacement of Javanese power from Central to East Java in the tenth century. These included the shifts of terms for domains from the early eighth- to thirteenth-century Java, which were possibly associated with the formative process and consolidation of its institutional body of polity: negara. Dhuwan was the ancient term for the contemporary word dukuh, meaning hamlet. Even though dhuwan was led by a kamitua, literally meaning someone who was highly respected as a senior person, its political organization had not yet been established and was not as sophisticated as wanua or desa.

Compared to dhuwan, desa was actually a borrowed Sanskrit word, literally meaning a sociopolitical institution of agricultural settlement that was traditionally under a negara’s hegemony. As a political organization, desa is economically supported by a self-sufficient agrarian community based on wet rice cultivation. The establishment of some desas was in many cases because of their political affiliation with a negara that was originally a wanua. The affiliation was made by a negara through political processes or military conquests.

The other form of affiliation was based on a political mechanism of territorial expansion of a negara. It was made with a land grant system to the state bureaucratic apparatus. The state ruler granted the land to the state officers for their domain or territory. Such domain or territory in ancient Java was known as lungguh. In the course of time, the populations of lungguh territory were growing and developing as an agricultural community and settlement. The political status of such settlements varied as to whether they were desa lungguh or kadipaten/kabupaten. This kind of settlement was provided with a seat of local ruler at its center. Kadipaten or kabupaten were regencies or subordinate states that were led by the princes of state (hino/mahamantri) or royal family members with high-rank state office status (pamgat).

In its mature form, an ordinary desa was a territorially organized entity of settlement with institutions for political, economic, and social affairs. The head of desa was demang, akuwu, or lurah, who was assisted by a deputy (carik), priests (resi, pengulu), and medicine men (dukun). He led villagers who principally consisted of landlords or patrons of work called gawe haji. In dealing with village affairs, every traditional village was provided with a village council called karaman consisting of landlords and elders. As a community, ancient Javanese desa had its own local customary laws (cara) for their community life.

Some ancient desa traditions still survive today. The most important event is the tradition of bersih desa. It is an event for cleaning up the village and for celebrating the rebirth of the community as the world. In ancient times such collective voluntarily work for a lord was called buncang haji. But today it is the same with a different name, known as gotong royong (carrying together) or gugur gunung (falling mountain). In ancient times and today, the leadership of the village chief is very important as the role of the father of the village community. In addition, for safety and security desa is provided with a night guarding system (ronda) on a voluntarily basis. Village night guards are mostly adult males who are pulled out from every household. They guard during the night and are provided with kentongan (bamboo instruments) that they strike if they need to alert the community to danger.

Although Hindu and Indic culture had introduced the Javanese society to the four castes (svawarna), the adoption had been subtly assimilated within the preeminent dualistic system consisting of noblemen and commoners. Small kingdoms in ancient Java had at least four state ranks: rakai (ruler), pamgat or samgat (state officer), haji (landlord), and samya haji (servant of landlord). Besides bureaucrats, state priests (pandita, resi, Begawan) and monks (bikhu, cantrik) played important roles and functions for state rituals and ceremonies while the merchants and traders (nayagan/niagawan) ran the agrarian economy with the support of commoners (paura) as workers of landlord (gawe haji) and laborers (katik).

From the inscriptions of the eighth to the tenth century, one noteworthy concept among others was associated with the ancient hamlet called dapur, literally meaning pioneer land for settlement. The terms dapur or dhapuk were used for the pioneer settlement after the site clearing of forest, called babad alas. The next stage of its development, dapur had to establish its domestication based on shifting rice cultivation (pagagan or gaga). The social unit of such pioneer settlement was known as dhuwan. Today such a concept is still in use with a similar name: dephok, meaning hamlet or a settlement with scattered houses.

The transformation of dhuwan to thani was made possible by the practice of agriculture known as sawah, through which the development of settlement occurred as an underlying form of domestic production. Dhuwan and thani as settlement could have been sociopolitically a part of wanua. Both terms are to articulate the modes of rice cultivation. They were settlement units either politically independent or in affiliation with an economically and politically established wanua.

Aksara, Sawah, and Negara

Literacy and wet rice cultivation were considered crucial and essential for the concentration of power and its formative state polity in Java and other parts of the archipelago. Most of the social and political concepts concerning the state have obviously been derived from Sankrit dharmasastra that is also the source of state rituals, statecraft, institutions, and iconography (Heesterman 1989, p. 5). The Sankrit literacy (aksara) had been introduced to the ruling class in Java and the outer islands by Buddhist monks (bikhu) and Brahmins (brahmana) from South India between 100 and 600 AD, while the practice of wet rice cultivation (sawah) could have been adopted from the Southeast Asian mainland around the first century. However, the oldest available inscription of Kutei was dated back not earlier than the fourth century.

Evidently, historical traces of early kingdoms of the fifth century were available in stone and cooper inscriptions in East Kalimantan and West Java. The script was still in Pallawa. According to living oral Javanese accounts, the literacy was taught by Prince Adji Sakha from South India to Javanese noblemen (Iguchi 2015, p. 78). From him the literacy and lunar calendar began in Java. It was dated back to circa 78 AD. Moreover, the prince was well known in Javanese oral tradition and taught the Javanese landlords the Devanagari script in Sankrit and Pallawa letters. Literacy was kept by and for the ruling class only and was conceived of as a celestial privilege. Since then, keeping Sanskrit texts in secrecy was the Javanese noble tradition. The relationship between India and Southeast Asia—especially Java—has always been unique in terms of cultural history. The cosmopolitan Sanskrit world has hardly been divided in terms of compartmentalization and regionalization that only leads us to lose sight of the natural and historical unity (Heesterman 1989).

One thing that ordinary people got the benefit from Sanskrit literacy was the calendar system. The Javanese lunar calendar (pawukon) then was developed by Prince Adji Saka. Since then, the year calendar system was named after him: taon saka. However, the influence of Sanskrit has been gradually integrated with local culture. As shown in the inscriptions of the twelfth century, Javanese negara did not use the Sanskrit language and Pallava script, but instead developed their own language, known as basa kawi and the script of hanacaraka aksara. Nevertheless, literacy in ancient Java was conceived as sacred knowledge and a skill so that it could have established the ruling class and the elite status in the state polity of negara. Sociologically speaking, literacy in ancient Java had made a strong social dichotomy between the ruling class and the ordinary people. Meanwhile, wet rice cultivation strengthened the position of the ruling class in dominating the commoners with a feudalistic system of economy.

Economically, the alteration from chiefdom to kingdom in ancient Java was not only made possible by the influence of Indic civilization, but also possibly by the provision of productive development in agricultural techniques, which were transformed from tegal (slash and burn farming) to thani (agricultural village or settlement unit). This happened possibly during the first through the seventh centuries. The origin of such techniques had been well known from the mainland of Southeast Asia, especially from the Mekong Delta in Vietnam (Wheatley 1983, p. 113; Needham et al. 2000, pp. 123, 486). Then wet rice cultivation and Indic statecraft had enabled the early Javanese wanuas to settle down in a location with a proto state political system based on agricultural cultivation in terms of thani.

The change of domestication in Java from shifting cultivation (negal) to farming (nyawah or ntani) was, however, not the only important factor for the transformation of dwelling culture from egalitarian community to hierarchical agrarian society. There must have been some other factors too, such as tool-making techniques. Thus, the period between the first and seventh centuries was also a time of agricultural evolution that enabled them to found a state society and polity. The transformation was, of course, a gradual process accompanied by the development of making tools based on metal or iron.

Blacksmith (gusali) and irrigation techniques (sawah) could have transformed and increased the value of land by the intensification of domestication that enabled the Javanese agriculture to reach a surplus of economic production. Meanwhile, exchange (nyaga) of commodities in the permanent market (peken) could have developed in the ancient Javanese society with various specialized skills that enriched their life with arts, leisure, entertainment, and social life. Then the period of the first to the seventh centuries could have been considered the formative time for the Javanese people in developing their agricultural society. Besides, it must have been the era when the surplus of domestic productions was challenged by the necessity of efficient management of land and water.

Indeed, the transformations in production systems and the changes in the political system came to happen simultaneously and reciprocally. The shift concept of settlement from wanua to thani was gradually made through the production of thani, literally meaning farming based on an irrigation system (sawah). The intensification of land and the highly sophisticated management of water were developed. Unlike the Balinese irrigation association (subak), Javanese peasants (thani) integrated their water management within the village assembly (karaman). Despite the fact that its names may vary across Javanese villages, its sociopolitical function as a community council is still in existence today. Coordinating and distributing water supply and mutual help for rice cultivation became an important part of the assembly’s gathering.

Of course, rituals and ceremonies related to rice cultivation were also the part of the assembly’s job. This includes consultations and assistance. The composition of ancient village council members was not only politicians and community leaders, but also experts and trade peoples. Spiritual experts (mpu or dukun) gave their advice and directions through the assembly for the best time for rituals and ceremonies of planting, maintaining, and harvesting rice based on the Javanese almanac (pewukon). The expert on the weather and calendar system was known as wariga. He was also an elder and member of village council.

Meanwhile, the indigenous social grouping of Javanese society, as well as other Austronesian descendants, was still in old fashion and previous form. They were not immediately open to Indic social stratification of caste (varna). Indeed, the celestial group of brahmins could have been accepted by them as a highly respected group in affiliation with the nobles (bhogi or priyayi). The noblemen needed the religious authority to legitimate their accumulation of power. Special status was given by the ruling class to Buddhist and Hindu priests as a reward for their spiritual support and knowledge on statecraft. In return, as taught by Hindu and Buddhist doctrines, donations to the poor and brahmana or bikhu were given by the ruling class as shown in the Karmawibangga gallery of Borobudur.

To what extent was the concentration of power possible in ancient time? Geographically speaking, the scattered inscriptions and monuments from various reigns in the regions of the Kedu plains, Dieng plateau, Brantas plains, and Malang region, where wet rice cultivation find its most favorable location, show us the field condition that consolidation of power was not easy. The situation of the past was dominated by steep and arduous topographical sites as well as dense tropical forests. The geographical setting confirms the fact that ancient rulers should have lived more coexistentially, rather than as a dominant conqueror and superior emperor. The consolidation of power under a single ruler could have not been easy without the support of monasteries. In the world of asceticism like in ancient Java, the ruler was seen as a powerful person, not only in terms of being a political leader, but also as a spiritually exceptional person.

How did the state concept of early negara develop in other parts of Indonesia and what were its consequences for urbanism? Hitherto, the traces and inscriptions concerning negara have been hard to find in Kutei of East Borneo from the first to the fifth century, but they have been revealed in West Java. The four inscriptions of Ciaruteun at Bogor from 450 AD written in Pallava script confirm the existence of three concepts of settlement related to the idea of state: negara, puri, and sibira. It is, however, less apparent or even confusing whether the meaning of the word sibira is pura or temple. Since the use of the concept in epigraphic evidence on Purnawarman of West Java is probably associated with holy place, the word is perhaps close to the Indonesian word vihara or biara, meaning monastery. Even though it is difficult to draw a clear picture of the early township in Java from archaeological findings, there are two constituent institutions of the Indic concept of negara in Tarumanegara from the Purnawarman’s inscriptions, namely sthana, the seat of ruler or palace, and pura, the center of state rituals.

Regarding its etymology, the word sibira is not a Sanskrit word; its possible meaning is close to pura, which is essential and decisive for the concept of negara. Pura was literally a holy place where state rituals and ceremonies took place. As urban institutions, puri and ksetra (the holy area for pura) could have constituted the idea of center. Despite their religious importance, they were essentially insufficient to constitute the idea of center if they were not supported by a permanent market. The indigenous market in ancient Javanese society was not only economically important, but also culturally significant as the real center of ancient Javanese life, as shown on the buried Borobudur’s Karmawibhangga panels. The ancient Javanese word for market was p’ken or peken, literally meaning gathering, interacting, exchanging, and coming together.

Then the emergence of township and statehood of negara would have been inconceivable without comprising the three essential institutions of centers for a state settlement: pura (the place of state rituals and ceremonies), puri (the seat of ruler), and peken (the place of economic exchange and socialization). Nevertheless, residential areas (paumahan), state institutions (sarana), and their infrastructure (prasarana), were important as well.

Even though in early negaras urban laws and codes for infrastructure and domain were not properly implemented, there were special codes and manners called tata krama that were applied for social interaction within the core of negara such as language (basa), dress/attire (busana), and manners (jawa). Such codes were not in use among villagers or rural people. According to the inscription of Mpu Ketudhara from 804, the consequences of violating codes and laws (tata karma) were executed in the court of justice (pakaranan) led by sang tuhan. Then fines and rewards were granted by the court for individuals with the status of citizen (warga negara).

The use of the three centers (pura-puri-peken) could have been relevant with the basic exigency for the establishment of urbanity in ancient Java. The focus of negara since the fifth century had always been the kraton or the center of political, cultural, and spiritual hegemony. Beyond the center, domains had always been only the parts of the agricultural production of desa. Accordingly, high-density settlement was not necessary for ancient Java because the center had not required them. What the center needed was the agricultural products, taxes, and tributes. In addition, the concentration of power in the Javanese tradition was not equal with the concentration of settlement because territorially the defense system of ancient negaras was distributed to each desa or lungguh of mancapat and not concentrated in the capital town.

The notion of settlement (paumahan) was mostly associated with special occupation of its inhabitants or the name of its landlord (bhogi of lungguh). Lack of archaeological excavations on the sites where the Tarumanegara’s inscriptions had been found have left us with no further trace on the ancient towns. The only trace we can see from Tarumanegara, beyond its inscriptions, is its name; it comes from the old Sundanese word tarum, meaning indigo plant. Tarumanegara must have been well associated with indigo. The use of indigo in ancient Javanese society was widely used to color silk and cotton textiles. Since the location of Tarumanegara’s inscriptions was far inland from the river Tarum, the question concerning the possible location of its capital state in the today rice field town of Karawang region between the fifth and seventh century remains open. The newly found Hindu-Buddhist candi complex at Batujaya—the villages of Segaran and Telagajaya—Karawang nearby the river Tarum—Citarum—might give us a clearer and more precise idea of the location of the early Hindu-Buddhist Kingdom in West Java (Manguin and Indradjaja 2011, pp. 113–36; Perpustakaan Nasional Republik Indonesia 2015).

Nevertheless, negara as a politically organized entity of dwelling had come into being in the form of feudal state or kingship. This implies that the value of land property for the cultivation of crops has been the subject of taxations and tributes. The increasing value of land by means of water management technique led people to develop more complex socially organized polity that enabled them to manage and control its crops and other resources for the development of cultural productions. All this was based on the patron-client relationship of landowners and workers.

The change from shifting cultivation to farming was politically followed by the incorporation of villages into a single landlord’s sovereignty system. Was this a consequence of the Indic statecraft and polity tradition? The need for the expansion of power was always there. The problem was, indeed, not simply to dominate others with a military supremacy, but also to maintain the control over others efficiently, with discipline and strong leadership. The Indic civilization proffered local rulers with military knowledge and tactics that were depicted in the Mahabharata and Ramayana. Both great books had been adopted by the Javanese ruling class. However, the dissemination of the text from the court to the ordinary people had been widely performed by storytellers and performers (dhalang) with their puppet play performances (wayang). The ancient Javanese version of Ramayana was kakawin Ramayana; it was the same story of Rama and Shinta but it had been transformed from epic fiction to lyric poem. The writer of this was unknown but the work was presumably written during the reign of Rakai Watukura Dyah Balitung circa 856 AD.

Society and Settlement

The origin of the Javanese concept of village alliance called mancapat must presumably have been deployed from the Indic wheel turner (mandala). In some inscriptions from Kedu and Temanggung between the eighth and tenth centuries mentioned the confederation of five or ten villages for defense and economic cooperation in terms of panaturdesa and pangasta desa. Beyond its mysticism, mandala was possibly deployed to secure the agricultural production and water management in dealing with drought, famine, and the dry season (paceklik). Based on the mandala concept, villages—usually not more than five—were economically organized with a regularly rotating market. Each village got a turn to hold the weekly market day, once for each of the number of the participating villages. In terms of culture, such villages established a representative body for extradition of criminals (maling, begal, rampok) and synchronization of their customary laws.

Despite numerous monumental candi, buildings and inscriptions are evidently standing and spreading out in Java and Sumatra. There are hardly any archaeological traces of physically urbanized settlement with strong streets and blocks structure plans to be found prior to the era of the Majapahit town of Trowulan (1305–1527) and Kediri (1042–1222). Even though the ancient sites of states had not been revealed with remnants of public infrastructure, their concepts appeared in many inscriptions such as retaining ponds (segara), drainages systems (kalen), ports (bandar), public cemeteries (astana pralaya), public baths (patirtaan, pancuran), and dams (waduk/bendungan) for agricultural irrigation.

Unlike ancient village settlements, which were mostly stable, pre-Islamic towns as the centers of negara were relatively unstable because of territorial competitions among each other. Although the rises and falls of negara affected the existence of their states and capitals, their desas mostly survived and sustained their agricultural communities. Despite mostly urban structure plans that could have disappeared for centuries, generations of some ancient villages and states still could have kept some domain names as they were in the past. Indeed, contemporary uses of land could have been adjusted and changed with other activities, but the ancient names might remain the same, such as alun-alun, peken/pekan, pendapa, keraton/kedhatuan, and so forth. Besides natural disasters (pralaya), displacements of settlements were mostly the case for leaving out the ancient sites in negligence. Based on the locations of the inscriptions and their text, the ancient sites of the capital state could have been estimated, such as Medang at Mamratipura, Matyasih, Kayumwungan, Talang Air, and Gondosuli at Parakan Temanggung for the Shailendra dynasty from the middle of the eighth to the ninth century. However, from the fields, it is difficult to recognize and locate precisely the ancient sites from their physical and topographical traces, which mostly have been transformed into rice fields and wilderness.

The area of kuta negara as the capital of state would have been divided into several domains (kalungguhan, lungguh), which were managed and controlled by high-rank state officers (pamgat, adipati, or bupati) as landlords. Far and away from the capital town and in rural areas, such domains and lands were entrusted by the adipatis or bupatis to their low-rank state officers, called bekels. However, in the capital town, the domains (lungguhs) of settlement created urban blocks, which were divided by the street (marga) for the movement of people in group on their horses or on their feet. Albeit several inscriptions did not mention the settlements in the ancient state capital, chronicles from China confirmed the importance of the seat of the ruler as symbolic center of the world. The core of the settlement called dalem or jro was actually buildings or pavilions within the rectangular plan wall system (turus/penyengker).

As mentioned in several inscriptions of Jurungan of 876, Poh of 905, and Pagumulan of 902, prior to the reign of Sindok, there were important institutions of urbanism in existence, such as for the chief of executive officer of noblemen (patih), state priest (purahita), territorial liaison (desaadhyaksa), attorney (adhyaksa), judge (upapatti), communicator (pangujar), archive/record (citralekha), finance (kabandaryan), and taxation (pangurang). Specialists in various occupations were also well known in ancient Javanese society, such as undahagi for master builder, hawang for ship captain, mapadahi for drummer, kicaka for dancer, awayang for puppet plays, and rawit for musician. Their specific expertise is signified with the prefix juru or tuha before their given name. There were various state officers with special tasks as well as wikekas (messenger between villages), hulu wras (officer of rice), tuha alas (officer for forestry), hulair (officer for irrigation), mapken (officer for market), mataman (officer for park and garden), tuha gusali (officer for blacksmiths), huluwuatan (officer for bridge), and mawatas (officer for border and boundary).

Indeed, one essential component of urbanism is the state record. Accordingly, the record system during the ancient Mataram Kingdom was written on various media such as lontar leaf (ripta), bronze plate (tamra), and stone (prasasti). In addition, the other sign of urbanism was the existence of an exchange unit for trade. In the ancient Javanese culture the currency was based on various commodities that included the gold unit (kati), water buffalo (suwarna), and rice bowl (masuya).

Furthermore, urban society in ancient Java was organized within various associations with specific terms of membership, aim, and scope. Elders gather together within the kamitua group with special tasks for consultation of community affairs while housewives pulled themselves in their rainata group for community mutual help and family care. Children were looked after by the community in the wadwa rarai organization for their early training and play group, meanwhile teenagers have their own association as well such as rara or werewereh for girls and wadwan matuha manwam for boys.

Economically, the state and urbanism needed revenue from various taxes (pajeg) and fines (sukha dukha). In the ancient Javanese Hindu-Buddhist Mataram era, revenues (pangguhan) were collected on an annual basis, consisting of property tax, husbandry tax, and rice cultivation tax. Taxation on a daily basis was collected from commercial transactions (masawyawahara) and crafts (mistral). According to Watukura’s inscription of 902, there were some other door-to-door contributions from people to the state on a regular basis for special occasions, such as the donation of flowers (pangraga skar) for the full month ceremony. State rituals and celebrations such as for sima were not only participated by local people, but also by foreigners and highly specialized skill persons (wargga kilalan), from Thailand (siam), Khemer (kmir), India (kling, dravida, arya), Sri Lanka (singhala), and representatives from other regions. The inscriptions never mention the Chinese as foreigners. Unlike local citizens (warganegara), all foreigners had to pay visa or domicile tax.

The Cult of Devaraja

Nevertheless, we have not yet found inscriptions from the eighth to tenth centuries that mention the likely Indic cult of devaraja from the Javanese rulers of Samaratungga, Daksa, Balitung, and Sindok. In his several inscriptions, even Sindok never declared himself with divine names such as Indra, Isvara, Wisnu, or Narendra. Instead, he used to call himself Rakryan Mahapatih or Rakryan Mahamantri, the indigenous word for chieftain with the highest authority. This shows us how the adoption of Sankrit Indic culture was very selective in favor of local pride and dignity. In other words, the Indic colonialization over Java or Indonesia was not the case in terms of political hegemony.

Embracing of Indic religion was not simply by reason of similarities of spiritual tradition between Hinduism and pantheistic Animism. Rather, the adoption of the Indic tradition was because of the syncretic tradition of Austronesian culture. Accordingly, any influence should be considered as the subject to be learned and be brought under control as a Javanese asset, based on the belief of acculturation, which is shareable among Javanese people as njawani of being Javanese. Being Javanese is literally being cultivated, tolerant, and aware of circumstances. The adoption of Hinduism and Buddhism must have had nothing to do with Indic colonialism. Instead, the Sanskrit culture had tremendously enriched the native culture and tradition with the improvements in trade, statecraft, and literacy.

Figure 2.7. A memorial visit by a group of girls to Caitya on the panel of Karmawibangga Borobudur from the eighth century. Photo courtesy of Rudi Badil and Katalis Publishing (Badil 1989: 66).

From Indic culture, the Javanese negara learned to apply the concept of mandala in the context of wheel turner (cakravartin) after the displacement of the center from Central to East Java, prior to the reign of Dharmawangsa. In order to deploy mandala, landlords had to struggle for their hegemony over others. Mandala was a metaphysical framework of negara as center in the cosmic constellation. It was actually for geographical defense against disintegration that a negara should be located at the center of four cardinal villages (desa). Perfect deployment of mandala required political and military supremacy. After having such supremacy, the support from religious clerics came into play for justification. Needless to say, esotericism, asceticism, and mysticism in the Javanese tradition have been part of the power establishment. In doing so, the rulers gained social detachment from their common people.

The motive for the establishment of religious monuments (candis) might have been related to the achievement of consolidating several negaras under one control. However, the Shailendra dynasty (770–825 AD) and the Sanjaya dynasty (910–929 AD) needed the support of the celestial group for their pride and achievement. In doing so, the rulers could demonstrate their personal ambitions under a religious umbrella. As a matter of fact, there was no evidence whatsoever from the inscriptions to confirm that monuments were the centers of religious life. Instead, the state rituals and ceremonies took place in the town square and at the seat of the ruler. Indeed, the monuments were visited by ancient people in terms of pilgrimage.

Even though sociopolitically Javanese society had achieved complexity and a highly sophisticated system of state polity, it was difficult to find physical evidences of settlement with highly concentrated populations. How could the sophisticated monuments like Prambanan (ca. 856 AD) and Borobudur (ca. 790 AD) complex have been built without a spatially organized place for living settlement of large populations? Despite perishable materials and the latent threat of volcanic eruptions of Mount Merapi and Mount Merbabu, concentration of settlements would have been necessary to build monuments with such a large scale that took years or decades. However, it could have nothing to do with the political center or capital town. Rather, the concentration was conceptually temporary for the construction of the monuments, but it was not intended for urbanism. Such settlement was dissolved after the projects were done. Then the question is: What was the relationship of negara and its monuments, such as Dharmatungga and Samaratungga of the Shailendra dynasty, Borobudur and Daksa of the Sanjaya dynasty, and Lara Jongrang and Prambanan? What actually was candi for the early Javanese states?

As mentioned earlier, the existence of negara as a spiritually unifying entity of its citizens (paura) did not exist without religious rituals (caru and puja). In order to experience the unity as an event, negara needed ceremonial and formal occasions in which collective union and commitment were displayed with splendid spectacle. The culmination of the event was about the self-gratification of the king or the queen, which was signified by the inauguration of his/her monument. The monuments in this regard were simply the vehicles of the rulers for immortalizing their legacy. More precisely, a candi for Javanese rulers was more about the altar of their power and legacy rather than their devotion to their people. The Javanese rulers were mostly too proud of themselves as the celestial representation on earth and did not care for people outside their palace wall but for tax, tribute, loyalty, their state rituals, and ceremonies.

Indeed, the presence of caitya was also symbolic as mandala or prasada. Candi as monumental architecture was visually more useful for orientation rather than for abstract religious purpose. As demonstrated in the panel of Borobudur’s Karmawibangga, caitya was not the meeting place for a congregation like a mosque or a church. Thus, the presence of caitya in ancient Java could have been simply a memorial structure (tugu), as perceived in the time prior to the Indic influence. Thus, a religious cult at the caitya must have ideologically sustained the indigenous beliefs, but it might have been restructured with Indic outfits and accessories.

Caitya in ancient Java was likely established as a sign of validation that avowed the relationship between negara and its citizens, as a place to proffer gratitude to gods and ancestors. In addition, caitya could have provided the ancient Javanese society with a new altar to replace their Animistic pepunden, a sacred site with ziggurat form. Rituals related to caitya must have reminded people of the identity with their ancestors. Being reminded of their same origin and ancestors must have been the motive of the ancient Javanese people to build their caitya instead of any other religious intention.

Figure 2.8. Candi Singasari with Shiva Buddha elements as embodiment of Kertanegara’s cult. Photo by Bagoes Wiryomartono.

For the ruling reigns, the establishment of caitya could have instituted the unity of negara (state) and its kawula/paura (citizens), which could enable them to mobilize their resources. Needless to say, negara without any religious-cultural affiliation seems to remain an abstract concept and untouchable. Since the sense of negara as power could be made concrete by monument building, caitya for ancient Javanese society could have provided them with a spiritually unified place that enabled them to collectively address their hope, adoration, blessings, and gratitude to something bigger than life.

After the reign of Sindok from the tenth century on, candis were evident as the monuments of the great rulers’ legacy; for instances, candi Gunung Kawi for Airlangga, candi Jago for Ken Arok, candi Kidal for Anusaphati (1227–1248), and candi Singasari for Kertanegara (1254–1292). The question arises: What is the relation of negara to the candis as the monumental buildings of negara?

According to Mula Malurung’s inscription plate V-a and V-b, there were several types of candis during the Singasari Kingdom: thani bala (monument of anti-bad luck), sima (freehold monument), kalang (artisan structure), Kalagyan (a village name), kamulan (shrine), Kakurugan (a village monument), Kuti (a village name), biara (monument of monastery), Sala (a village name), parahyangan (place of worship), and karesian (spiritual memorial structure). The contribution of Singasari to the architecture of candi is obviously a reinterpretation of its function as the ruler’s monument. The reigns of Singasari from Ken Arok to Kertanegara have transformed the function of candi from sacral and religious monuments to rulers’ memorial buildings.

Since the displacement of Javanese Kingdoms from Central to East Java and especially after Singasari, the king’s legacy had been dignified with a monumental structure (Muljana 2006, p. 64). Given that power in Javanese culture was cosmic energy (kesekten), the cult of the ruler was immortalized with a monument that could have been the way of preserving this ascetic power. Although from the reign of Majapahit we have not found any large-scale monument, the last great Hindu emperor in the archipelago gave us the traces of urban settlement in Java. The remnants of Majapahit in Trowulan give us the most vivid example of a Javanese capital town where multilayered domains of center and highly sophisticated land use must have been deployed. Even though some candis at the Trowulan site could have been dedicated to its rulers, this devotion seems not to have been intended for their tombs or mausoleums. Most ashes of the rulers were sent to the ocean through the flow of the adjacent river.

As mentioned earlier, the foreign influences have been mostly adopted by the Javanese culture in a syncretic way. However, some cases of local pride and dignity play an important role in the acculturation process. This was the case that had been shown by the Queen Suhita (1416–1459 AD) with her monument, candi Sukuh. She strongly resisted the spread of Islam in the fifteenth century. As the last empress of Majapahit, she built the candi with indigenous Javanese architecture that reinvented native built form prior to the influence of Indic culture. The design of candi Sukuh was based upon a ziggurat-like form or terraced sanctuary, umpak pepunden, originally with a lingam-yoni at its peak. Four stone ladders of the candi were designed to lead people from the ground to the top of the building with its rectangular pinnacle.

Considering the decorative motifs of relief and sculpture embellishing the temple, an amalgam of late Hindu-Buddhist influence and Javanese megalithic monument was obviously carved. The form of the candi reminds people of the altar of ancestral cult in the Malayan-Javanese indigenous pantheistic Animism. Moreover, the sculptural elements and decorations of candi Sukuh depict the Javanese interpretation of characters of Mahabharata and Hindu-Buddhist gods with caricaturist and comedic scenes.

The establishment of the states could be seen as the institutionalization of the personal cult of their rulers. It was politically accomplished after he had attained the sovereignty of power. However, the status of political hegemony was not complete without religious justification and public spectacle. Ritually, inaugurating himself as super king, maharatu cakrawati needed support from spiritual leaders that enabled him to perform his theater of power.

Figure 2.10. Statue of Airlangga depicted as Wisnu from the eleventh century, Collection of Museum National Indonesia. Photo by Bagoes Wiryomartono.

Religious rituals and ceremonies were the means and vehicle of power show off for self-gratification that the king was the god on earth. Religious processions and services were an effective way to gather public attention and participation with respectful, intentional, and solemn acts. Of course, public ecstasy was the peak of all these rituals and ceremonies that signified the dramatic event with a single resolution for the king only. It was the time when the ruler announced his new name with celestial title, such as hyang, hulun, indra, narendra, mahadewa. All this signified the personal cult of ruler as the representation of absolute authority on earth. In doing so, the king was conceived as an ascetic being and socially distanced himself from his people. Such a metaphysical distance created the sense of the king as a mysterious figure. The detachment worked well to terrorize and terrify people who wanted to challenge his hegemony. The purpose was clearly to make his people absolutely respect and adore him as absolute ruler. Needless to say, it is the easy way to maintain his hegemony and power as absolute ruler. The first encounter with the phenomenon of the cult of devaraja is evident with the statue of Airlangga depicted as incarnated Wisnu on garuda.

Evidently, the rise and fall of candis as monuments of the rulers had been always in concert with the rise and fall of their states. The candis of the Shailendras and the Sanjayas in Central Java were drastically abandoned for centuries after its center of negara had been relocated to Kediri East Java by Sindok in 911 AD. Although the available inscriptions mention the relationship between states and their candis, there is no any specific clue on their functions and services as places of worship. In most cases, candi for Javanese people had always been a memorial structure (petilasan) where people paid homage and tribute (sesajen) to their ancestors with incense (dupa) and foods on a regular basis.

Based on the records of Fa-hien, a Chinese traveler who was in India from 399 to 414, the architecture of temples in Java reached its finest achievement. During the Guptas reign, arts, literature, and architecture could have dispersed to Java and Sumatra through the missionary works of Nalanda Brahmins and Budhist monks (bikhu). One of the most famous Buddhist scholars in Java was a Kashmir Gunavarman, who might have been working in Java until 421 AD before he continued his work in China until his death in Nanking in 467.

Power and Center

In Java as well as in most parts of Indonesia, town in terms of negara is distinguished from village or hamlets because of two essential physical components; a permanent market and the seat of authority. Both institutions were crucial structures of a Pre-Islamic negara in Indonesia that defined the centre of negara politically and economically as well. The coexistence of the kraton and the peken determined the existence of the capital town of the state (kuta negara). Nonetheless, both institutions of centre did not have a permanent or monumental structure.

Nevertheless, it was mostly not the case that the ancient Javanese rulers built their own palaces with stones. They could do so but they did not build permanent structure and buildings beyond temples and monuments. Prior to pre-Islamic states, the seats of the rulers disappeared along with their states. Obviously, the remnants of Ratu Baka from the eighth century invite us to speculate that the location could have been the site of the ancient Javanese palace. Indeed, the remains of Ratu Baka site gave us more evidence that there were permanent buildings beyond candis. Given that the remains of the complex are strategically situated on a hill, this fact could disqualify our speculations because most kratons in the Javanese tradition were always on the plain and flat area, which is not on the isolated hill and surrounded by woods instead of by desas.

Figure 2.12. Airlangga Shrine, Gunung Kawi Tampak Siring Bali from circa 1037 AD. Photo by Bagoes Wiryomartono.

According to Abhayagirivihara’s inscription, dated back to 792 AD, the site was associated with vihara of Rakryan Panangkaran of Sanjaya dynasty. Abhaya Giri Vihara means monastery on a peaceful hill. It should be a Buddhist monastery or a retreat place for the former rulers in devoting their life for spiritual purposes. Based on the site plan, the complex shows Hindu and Buddhist elements—Durga, Garuda, Ganesha, Yoni, and Lingga, as well as three unfinished Dhayani Buddha—all together with pools for ritual bathing (paseban) and sanctuary caves for meditation. On the site the two linggas called hara bear the date of 778 Saka or 886 AD. In addition, the linggas’s inscription mentions their erection under the order of King Kalasobhawa. The unison between Buddhist and Hindu faith is obvious on the inscription with text: Om Rudra ya namah swaha that adores Shiva with the Buddhist way.

The Javanese idea of center is indeed not only expansively radiating control, but also metaphysically absorbing every world to the self of the ruler. This implies that acknowledging the ruler’s supremacy and hegemony is not only politically in agreement, but also spiritually in submission. The accumulation of authority is equal with the absorption of power. As found in the inscriptions of Rukam (ca. 907 AD), the idea of state was articulated in terms of the realm of self (wanua i rukam wanua jro) (Boechari 1976). Subsequently, the whole idea of state (negara) is the self (dalem, jro) as expandable circle of radiating light (teja) of the center of power.

Indeed, the seat of the king is symbolically located at the innermost part of negara’s realm. But the expansive capacity of Javanese ruling power is not always territorial, but also cultural in terms of state rituals and ceremonies in which all people of his/her kingdom present and gather in submission to the splendid spectacle of the ruler’s show. Ultimately, the self of the ruler is the essence of the Javanese home that asserts expansively to surmount others under his/her hegemony. All this is dramatically performed by his/her state rituals and ceremonies as a splendid spectacle.

Still, it was probably the same reason why Purnawarman of West Java in his inscription, besides declaring himself as lord of landlords (parthiwa indra) announced his gratitude to the brahmins, who were from the early Indian Gupta empire (Reily 2012, p. 116), for the endorsement to his kingship. In return to the donation of land the priests had given their support for the celestial eminence and the establishment of state rituals; all this was for the cult of the king as the god of Isvara or Wisnu. Therefore he could build special monument for his cult and legacy. Purnawarman legacy was inscribed on the stone with his foot imprints.

Architecturally speaking, self-gratification and claim for individual cult as absolute ruler had probably begun with the reign of Sindok in East Java during the eleventh century. More precisely, it was Airlangga who mythologized himself as Wisnu on earth. His memorial building is located in Gunung Kawi Tampak Siring Bali. As mentioned by Ngimbang’s inscriptions, Airlangga was successful in concentrating his power in 1037 AD and qualified for cakrarvartin status (Pigeaud 1960, p. 470). Since then, the concentration of power in Kediri and Singasari was demonstrated by the state ritual of the entitlement of ruler as ratu cakrawati of the lord of the spiritual as well as the secular world as a whole.

Kota or kuta was originally a Sanskrit loan word articulating a fortified domain. The wall (turus) had been more for symbolic purposes than for practical use because it was traditionally neither provided with military barracks and warehouses for logistics and weapons nor with a concentration of settlements. Traditionally, the wall had been conceived by Javanese people as the structure of dalem, literally meaning the self. Probably the palace and the capital of the state were not developed for large and dense populations because it was not required. The center was conceived as the place of splendid spectacle that happened only occasionally, such as for sima rituals and ceremonies (Munoz 2006, p. 348).

Concluding Remarks

Although there had been few archaeological excavation of historic sites of potential urbanism in Java and other parts of Indonesia, prior to the period from the fifth to the tenth century, the potentiality for the concentration of settlement was obvious. However, it did not happen in terms of a concentration and highly populated settlement. Instead, a concentration was the case in terms of court culture. It was indeed with a highly sophisticated system of societies that supported court rituals and ceremonies. Although the center of political and economic power had not been demonstrated by a grand urban architecture, the splendid spectacle of state rituals and ceremonies were in demand, which took place in the square of the capital town. Did the ancient Javanese culture understand urbanism as a grand event instead of great urban form with concentration of settlement?

Despite the lack of physical evidence, ancient concepts related to urban phenomena did exist with kuta negara as its main system. The concepts that are essential for negara as state and urban core are hyang/pura (religious and sacred center), puri (seat of ruler/palace), peken (permanent market), lungguh (urban block/domain), marga (street), kreteg (bridge), and kalen (drainage). In contrast to the village (wanua/dukuh), the emergence of town (kuta) must have established the center of several villages (desa) for its political and military territory as well as its socioeconomic region.

The thesis as argued by Kulke (1991) that in ancient Java prior to the period before the tenth century were states without cities is barely acceptable and considered a hasty conclusion. Accordingly, urbanism is not simply about physical structure, but rather it is about culture. In the case of the Javanese experience, judging urban settlement without mentioning the relationship between urban structure and the concept of power and state is historically inadequate because the sense of urbanism lies in its political institution of state system. Lack of physical evidence for urban infrastructure is not a solely determinant factor in the Javanese and Indonesia context because most building elements of settlement in this tropical land are subject to perishable materials. Only stones and terracotta are used for monumental buildings. This fact leads us to scrutinize aspects of urbanism beyond its physical structure. Since the essence of urbanism is conceptually for being urbane, then the Javanese people understand this concept as karma (codes of courtesy and manner) in terms of alus (culturally cultivated). The center of kuta negara has always been associated with the place where cultural cultivation and fine manners have been practiced. In the Javanese context, urban and non-urban or rural areas are distinguished not by their physical structure, but by their conducts of civility. Most Western scholars might have overlooked these criteria in understanding what urban in Javanese culture is.

Chapter Three

Kuta Negara

The Javanese Idea of City/State and Its Historical Evolution

In spite of its origin from the Indic civilization, negara as a concept of state and statecraft has been adopted, adjusted, and modified by Javanese culture in various ways and fashions. Throughout the centuries, the Javanese negara has been developing with its own way and style of dealing with urbanity and civilization. This chapter argues that the Javanese urbanism is a center without definite periphery. The approach of this chapter is hermeneutical and conceives the urban world as the text of locality. The chapter will examine and scrutinize local concepts related to the phenomena of urbanity and settlement. The purpose is to unfold and dismantle their constituent relations and structures of meanings. The materials of the chapter were carried out from author’s experience as a native Javanese, who completed intensive fieldwork in Java between 1993 and 2010.

Urban Settlement in Javanese Culture

The history of urbanism in Java is inevitable to deal with the fact that urban form in this culture is probably not defined by its economic potentiality, but by its ritual and symbolic purpose. Studies by Wertheim (1980), Evers and Korff (2000, pp. 25–37), Bishop, Philips, and Yeo (2003), and other scholars—McGee (1967) and Ginsburg (1955)—confirm that the idea of urbanism in Southeast Asia is theoretically puzzling though its urban form has two main patterns: the inland states and harbor principalities. Hitherto, such theories came from the Western point of view. Wertheim’s notes on Peter J. M. Nas’s The Indonesian City remind us that the study needs to listen to the Indonesian side on their cities and society (Wertheim 1987). This chapter is in response to Wertheim’s call and an attempt to disclose and unfold the evolution of state and city in Java from inside, with specific reference to local concepts, texts, and artefacts.

The other reason to write this chapter is the fact that urban studies with focus on urban form and culture in this region fall short of publication. While most scholars on Indonesian urbanism are largely right in their analysis and theories on uniquely patrimonial and despotic cities and towns in Southeast Asia, they seemingly overlook some important aspects of the relationship between urban form and the local political culture on Java, which is preserved and conserved in native language.

To a certain extent, urban culture comprises a system of ideas, values, principles, and expectations that works in its society in various forms, media, and manners. In order to be collectively sharable, urban culture needs to be communicative. Culture is a kind of text that is necessary to be read and to be depicted according to locality (Geertz 1983, p. 70; Geertz 1973, pp. 11–12). This chapter argues that the intentionality of the Javanese city/state lies in its concept of power in terms of spiritual center. Accordingly, city and state are conceptually interchangeable depending on how the center appears phenomenally as form or event. Under the notion of intentionality (Searle 1983), this chapter perceives the city as the world toward which and by which people are (Kulke 2001) a politically and culturally constructed reality of Javanese locality. Besides archaeological artefacts, historical texts, and local customs and traditions, the primary source of contextual readership of this construction is the local concepts, their meanings, and their context. However, analysis and interpretation is focused on the relationship between concepts and phenomena that construct and establish the things and events of the Javanese city/state.

Although historical studies on early states and cities in Java Indonesia have been made by several scholars (Coedes 1944/1968; Lombard 1980; Nas 1986; Rutz 1987; Wisseman-Christie 1986), misunderstandings are possibly made, in some cases because local concepts are mostly untranslatable in other languages. A further investigation is still needed to bring the meanings of local concepts into the light, which is supported with more local materials on urbanism in the Indonesian context. Indeed, most scholars on Indonesian urbanism are largely right in their analysis and theories on uniquely patrimonial and despotic cities and towns in Southeast Asia. However, they seemingly overlook some important aspects of local political culture that is preserved and conserved in the native language.

Wisseman-Christie (1991) argues demographically that settlements in pre-colonial Java prior to the twelfth century were states without cities. How were they possible? The question still lingers today. Since state and urbanism are historically indivisible as a consequence of the concentration of power, the phenomena of town and city are inseparable from the local concepts of hegemony and political culture. Wisseman-Christie’s conclusion that ancient Javanese kingdoms were states without cities could have been incomprehensive without examining and scrutinizing the Javanese idea of power and its local concepts. The concentration of power does not need a centralization of people and their settlements in one place. In Javanese tradition the concentration of power is more about the spiritual and symbolic seat of cosmic energy; the sense of center of power is experienced as a mysterious and secretive place. The more one comes closer to the core of power, the more one experiences its enigmatic aura and environment.

Although between the ninth and early thirteenth centuries Javanese populations were dispersed, their kingdoms achieved highly elaborate and strictly fixed hierarchy of state officers and local authorities (Kulke 2001, p. 16). The question remains open concerning spatial structure and domains. How was such erudition architecturally incorporated in their land use plan? The question leads us to investigate what is negara in early Javanese kingdoms, and during the Islamic, colonial, and republican eras.

Regarding Indonesian urban culture, there is likely nothing more important than the political institution of negara because the notion includes urban, center, city, and state. From ancient time until today, negara and kuta or kota were indivisible concepts that pertain to the center of power. Properly speaking, negara is a politically and culturally constructed reality of town and city in Java as well as in Indonesia.

Since the fifth century, there were more than hundreds and might be thousands negaras that have arisen and fallen in the archipelago. Most of them disappeared without physical traces, such as Pakuan, Padjajaran, Galuh, Medang, Blambangan, Early Mataram, and Kahuripan. Their presence in the past is barely known in textual evidence. Inscriptions (prasasti), legends (dongeng), annals (babad), and a few monuments (petilasan) are mostly biased because they are inevitably prone in support of the status quo.

It is true that the institution of negara is literally untranslatable in any other languages beyond the Southeast Asian experience or more specifically the Malay-Indonesian context. The originally Sanskrit loanword negara has been transformed and elaborated by Javanese as well as Malay cultural experience, which is not simply palace, capital, town, state, or realm. Clifford Geertz has perfectly described it in the broadest sense of the word as civilization that the city supported and the superstructure of political subordination centered there (Geertz 1980, p. 4).

Early Urbanization and the Emergence of Negara

The domain of negara in Javanese tradition is not marked by physical nature or by architecture but by various levels of language practice that identify its citizens as krama. The obvious structure is only revealed at the innermost part of negara. The word krama is derived from Sanskrit meaning manner, way, fashion, step, performance, and, in the broadest sense, citizen, within the domain kadatwan, where the court language krama-inggil was practiced. The formative component of negara was physically designated with a fortified square domain called in pura in Sanskrit, where the elites or the ruling group, priyayi, pangreh praja, lived. Such a domain in the old Javanese language has been called jero or dalem, meaning inner space, interior, core, center, and the self.

As a matter of archaeological fact, there is no negara without jero or dalem. The concept of jero is center; it is the seat of the landlord. Spatially, negara in the Javanese context is always a multilayered system of domains with jero or dalem at its innermost center. In many cases, the jero must have been fortified with a surrounding wall (beteng penyengker). Indeed, such a wall is still in existence in today’s kratons. It is more for a symbolic purpose than for defense. Astonishingly, it is difficult to find a definitive structure of negara’s periphery. Even though the capital town of negara in the Javanese tradition, or perhaps also in other Indonesian states from Aceh to Tidore, does not have any trace of definitive periphery and architectural boundary, its core is architecturally apparent in the form of a fortified enclosure called jero, jeru, dalem, or kraton.

The domains of negara are built around the court. The areas are laid out along main roads (marga raya) to state squares (alun-alun). The obvious enclosure (mbeteng) is not only manifesting at pura, kedaton, or kraton, but also in some domains (lungguh) where state officers live. Kedatwan or the domain is on what the castle (pura) stands. The other equivalent name with kedatuan is kajeruan, from the word jero, meaning the innermost part of negara. Even though they have similar meanings, in most Malay-speaking regions, kajeruan is less common than kedatuan.

The word kedatwan or kedatuan is actually derived from the ancient Malay word datuk, meaning the head/chief or lord of community. Thus, kedatwan is to articulate the seat of the chieftain. Datu in the pre-Hindu and pre-Buddhist phase is the chief of the village settlement called wanua. The Austronesian wanua is a patrimonial community with dualistic social groups—noble class and commoners. The center of polity is a village council consisting of noble leaders and elders. The ancient trace of wanua settlement is still alive today, such as the banua in South Nias and the kabisu in the Sumba islands.

Under the influence of Indic culture and civilization, the chief must have transformed the community within the pyramidal cosmic model of Mount Meru with him as its apex. The necessity for the concentration of power and authority must have been incarnated with the cult of Siva and Wisnu. Without this cult, the ruler is not metaphysically mysterious and sacred, set aside from his noble circle and ordinary people. Then secrecy, formality, and reserve have come into play for the devaraja cult established by rituals and ceremonies that mystify his self-gratification and his self-appointed celestial splendor of divinity.

Even though the development of the concept of kedatwan or kedaton has been deployed within the area of the ancient village of wanua, the adoption of Sanskrit in the Javanese and Malay polity had been very selective. They did not employ the Indic version from the word raja (king) prior to the period from the fifth to the tenth century. Nevertheless, the Indic negara for the notion of state remains in Sanskrit, though the meaning has been adjusted and elaborated with local circumstances. Prior to the reign of Airlangga in Kahuripan, the seat of the ruler has been pronouncedly transformed from the Malay word kedatuan or kedaton to the Javanese kraton, without a change in its meaning. Since then, the seat of the ruler had not been called pura.

Pura as the center of the seat of the ruler in the Javanese tradition seems have been translated into kraton. The transformation of the concept must have happened in the time when the Javanese culture and tradition began to translate various Indic cultures and literatures into its own language. The kings, such as Dharmawangsa, Airlangga, and Jayabaya, were well known in Javanese culture as the initiators for such Indic-Javanese acculturation. Today, several Javanese towns still carry the name of the domain and place indicating the corresponding area of kraton, even though its courts disappeared a long time ago, such as Kediri, Jepara, Demak, Plered, Kotagede, and Kartasura.

Mancapat as Spatial Framework of Center

From Indic state tradition, the Javanese negara learned to apply the concept of mandala in the context of wheel turner (cakravartin). The origin of the Javanese concept of the peasant village alliance called thani with the mancapat scheme must presumably have been deployed from the Indic wheel turner, mandala. Beyond its mysticism, mandala was possibly deployed to secure the agricultural production and water management in dealing with drought, famine, and the dry season (paceklik). Based on the mandala concept, villages were brought in to cooperative units of settlement called thani, with a regularly rotating market once per five-day week. Synchronization of customary laws and mores called cara must have been established that could have stabilized peace and security among villagers.

Mandala means essence or content in the original Sanskrit. The use of mandala in terms of kuta negara is to signify the idea of mediator between the mundane and the sacred. The purpose of this signification is to bring about the experience of mystical unity from which the manifold forms and appearances arise. The adaptation of mandala of Hindu into the Javanese culture is articulated in the concept of mancapat. Literally, it is the concept of realm beyond four corners and the four main cardinal directions: north, south, east, and west. Mancapat is the axis mundi between earth and sky. The word manca means beyond or further than, whereas the word pat is from papat or four.

The Javanese culture develops the mandala in a similar way to the Hindu origin, with an adjustment here and there according to Javanese cosmology. The rings or layers differentiate values of influence in relation to the source of power. In terms of kuta negara, the innermost part of the mancapat is the Kedaton or Dalem. The core is the residence of the king or sultan. The distinctive architectural form and spatial layout is apparently expressed with the palace inner wall called cepuri. Moreover, the buildings inside the inner wall system are distinctive in style, size, and elaboration of details. However, the most important thing is that this is the only building with main entry from the east. The main access is similar to the entry design of Hindu the temple (candi).

The second boundary of the mancapat is sentana dalem, which is understood as the realm of the court area for royal families, officers, and servants. It is the realm of cosmic power where the meeting of the ruler (gusti) and the ruled (kawula) is. The place where the meeting between the king/queen and his/her people is denoted with the building of bangsal witana. The wall system called baluwerti stands to demarcate the second area of the royal palace.

The third boundary of the mancapat concept is called kuta negara, which is understood as the extension of the center in all directions. In order to establish the realm of negara, the ruler and his followers had to open the forest for settlement and agriculture, or to seize control of existing villages and hamlets and to claim all these under his land ownership. As a landlord, the ruler protects and controls villages and hamlets with a taxation system (upeti/upetya) and payment of religious homage (sowan dalem kaprabon).

The fourth is the area of influence beyond the ruler’s land ownership, but under his/her protectorate system. The leaders of the protectorate region had to pay annual tribute (upeti) and to be present in state ceremonies (upacara negara). The protectorate region is the extension of his influence in terms of power (adidaya), charisma (wibawa), and reverence (wisesa) beyond his home seat or his own land. The areas are usually under other local rulers or his followers. The area is called negaragung (great state) and can be flexibly larger or smaller depending on the leadership and personal power of the ruler. Charismatic and strong leadership will increase the scope of spiritual influence. The boundary of negaragung is not always geographical territory (Miyazaki 1988, pp. 30, 164, 176; Laksono 1986, p. 64). It is actually constituted by acknowledgment of several landlords or regional rulers to the leadership of the ruler in terms of spiritual and political reverence.

The fifth area is mancanegara, meaning beyond the ruler’s influence or abroad. The area of mancanegara is unlimited in number and location. However, mutual respect with exchange of ambassadors and trades is possible. The mancanegara is the concept of articulating the idea of the outside world or beyond the negara’s rule and order. The expansionist move of negara into the realm of mancanegara is in any chance possible. It has nothing to do with merely the political influence. Rather, it is about the extension of the cosmological realm of Javanese culture. Most Javanese rulers are historically expansionist in the need to affirm their cosmological seat as the representation of the supreme being on the earth. The Javanese people think and believe in their messianic mission that their culture is to javanize other people outside their realm. To javanize means to civilize according to Javanese values. In their delicacy, being Javanese is to adopt and practice the Javanese custom and tradition in terms of mature, polite, subtle, and refined.

However, the border or boundary of mancapat in the context of negara is neither laid out with physical landmark nor with written document. The boundary is the event that is marked out by the use and practice the gradation of language, which is subtly oppressive in establishing the master/slave relationship. The Javanese people know the boundary in terms of addressing people they talk to. It is the event when respect and adoration are exercised.

Negara as the Stage of the Ruler Cult

Besides perishable materials and natural disasters—floods, earthquakes, and volcanic eruptions—the lack of intensive archaeological excavations in Java is one of the reasons for the difficulty in figuring out the early town-like structure of ancient Javanese settlements. The other reason lies in its conceptual basis that settlement remnants have not yet reflected the idea of negara as a political body. Ancient states might have been in a struggle of competition to establish perfect negara prior to Airlangga in the Kahuripan kingdom. It might be the reason why the ancient rulers until Mpu Sindok of the Medang kingdom had not yet successfully performed their cult of devaraja (Thakur 1986, p. 33; Vic 2005, p. 89).

The cult of Indian Devaraja based on Tantric Sivaism could have been in effect in the Javanese concept of the ruler as part of Javanese adoption of Indic state polity. However, locally customized adoption could have modified the rituals and meanings. The concept of Gusti to address God and ruler is the apparent proof for the cult. Cosmologically, the Javanese concept of ruler holds that the king is the representative divinity on earth. Indeed, rulers of Javanese Hindu Kahuripan’s Airlangga as well as Majapahit’s Kertarajasa are carved and eternalized as Wisnu while Anusapati of Singasari is carved as the embodiment of Siva. Their monuments are nothing but an architecturally immortalized claim of representative divinity in the sense of manunggaling Gusti-kawula (blending unity of immortal and mortals). The concept of kawula is hard to translate. It is not simply about the ruled as servant or subordinate. The idea of Gusti-kawula is also not quite similar to the relationship between the shepherd and the sheep, but master and slave (Peacock 1973, pp. 29, 158). Kawula is not only labor but also spiritual supporter, worshipper, follower, and believer, with fully submissive devotion. The unity of the ruler and the ruled is conceived by the Javanese culture—which is shaped and established by the status quo—as the reality of the world.

Town in the Javanese language is kuta, literally meaning land or settlement surrounded by a wall. In reality, the walled territory only exists at its core in which the ruler resides. The sense of kuta is characterized by the seat of the state ruler or the king, which is perceived as the center of the cosmic system on the earth. Kuta negara is the capital or the center of the state realm in terms of cosmological and spiritual sense. It is to protect human settlements in the metaphysical sense by utilizing the illiteracy of people outside the court wall.

As a cosmic center, kuta negara is a symbolic and spiritual source from which the authority and order radiates. Even though the core of kuta negara is an architecturally walled system of the ruler’s seat, the very idea of kuta negara is not always a territorially concentrated settlement. On the contrary, it is a borderless realm with a mandala imprint form at its center. Thus, the walled core of kuta negara has nothing to do with defense or security in the first place. It is originally a symbolic marking out of the cosmic center on earth as well as the radiance of the king’s power.

As described in several inscriptions, such as Mulawarman’s Yupa and Purnawarman’s sanghyang tapak, the existence of pura has been articulated as the place of the center of state rituals and ceremonies, where offering and donation take place. In addition to pura, Purnawarman’s inscription enables us to read the existence of puri, meaning the residency of the king and his royal family (praja). Pura and puri are institutions of negara that are still alive in Balinese tradition as constituent structures of a rectangular surrounding wall domain, kuta, from which grand ritual performances, upacara, are delivered.

Evidence has recently appeared from Indian and Chinese records that the earlier kingdom in Java was Salakanagara or Rajatapura from 150 to 358 AD; the founder of the kingdom was Dewawarman from Pallava, India, and married the daughter of local ruler Aki Luhur Sang Mulya (Iguchi 2015, p. 116). The location of the kingdom was at Teluk Lada, today called the Pandeglang area. The son-in-law of Dewawarman VIII, Jayasinghawarman, founded Tarumanegara; Purnawarman was his grandson, who reigned over forty-eight vassal kingdoms between 395 and 434 AD, from Sakalanagara in West Java to Purwalingga, today called Probolinggo in East Java. Despite its prosperous record on various inscriptions, archaeologists have not yet found the traces and urban relics of kuta negara for Salakanagara and Tarumanegara.

However, the most vivid evidence of such a fortified domain is obvious at most of the candis in Java, such as Candi Prambanan or the Lara Jongrang complex. Since most building materials for puri are made of perishable materials, mostly made of timbers, we are not able to see the coexistential relationship between pura and puri architecturally. In the Javanese tradition, the word khita used to be deployed for the domain where tata krama must necessarily have been practiced. From old Javanese sayings we hear desa mawa cara, negara mawa tata, meaning the village community is provided with customs and mores, whereas state has its own laws and orders (Miyazaki 1988, p. 26).

In contrast to rural vicinity (desa), khita/kuta or urban vicinity must have been marked with a conceptual boundary where urban language (krama) and polite conduct (tata) must have played an important role. Going into khita or negari for Javanese people is always an engagement with full respect and softly spoken language. In the Malay tradition, kota has been associated with any conduct and behavior of being humble (melayukan diri) (Rahman et al. 2003, p. 5; Sinar 1971/1986, p. 25). The boundary of urbanity marked by highly sophisticated language and manners in the Javanese tradition has comprised the concept of tata krama, meaning set of values and principles of conduct based on hospitality, respect, and humility.

The traces of ancient khita as shown by the remnants of Trowulan of the fourteenth to sixteenth centuries gives us a boundary covering ten square kilometers. Concentration of populations has been indicated with the remnants of terracotta foundations, networks of drainage, paths, and monuments within the area of khita. Even though the Majapahit capital Trowulan has not been inhabited since the end of the seventeenth century, traces of drainage and water reservoirs are still readable on site. In spite of Singasari and Trowulan leaving us their past reality in scattered sources of artefacts and texts, which are due to be verified with archaeological excavations and historical texts, their essential structures and principles are still decipherable in today’s Javanese capital towns such as Cirebon, Yogyakarta, and Surakarta. Majapahit Trowulan has already shown the diversified domains of Javanese towns. The domains have been developed according to their specific relation to the court for cultural, bureaucratic, and weaponry professions and to the market (peken or pasar). Several domains of town are closely associated with the name of their landlords.

Locating the center in Javanese Hindu-Buddhist towns such as Kediri, Singasari, and Trowulan is indispensably associated with the emergence of the town square (alun-alun). In spite of the origin of alun-alun prior to the twelfth century, its conceptual root is Old Javanese, not Sanskrit. Hitherto, the available trace of the town square is Singasari’s alun-alun from the thirteenth-century nearby town of Malang. This might be the oldest available town square in Java. The alun-alun is today only recognizable from its toponymical trace. Its location is between the statues of Buddhist Dwaraphala and the candi Singasari. Although the square might have been associated with the center of settlement, there is no architecturally indicated remains of Singasari’s kraton nearby. Entering the place within the enclosure such as the Buddhist temple palace is necessarily making a round walk ritual clockwise in front of the guardian statues. Presumably, the emergence of alun-alun was originally to accommodate such a ritual. As a matter of fact, Singasari’s alun-alun is smaller than Yogyakarta’s town square. Today the historic site of Singasari’s square has been transformed into a road crossing area.

Remarkably, there is the spatial arrangement of alun-alun, which is provided with candi and the gate guarded by two Dwaraphala giant figures. The north direction is the main entry direction to alun-alun, whereas the Saivite temple is located on the west with its front façade facing east. Such a sacred place denoting center in ancient India has appeared in the seal of Mohenjo Daro and coin from the Vedic period was signified with a tree enclosed by fence (Snograss 1985, p. 153).

In the Javanese tradition as described in Pararaton and Negarakertagama, alun-alun was characterized by a couple of banyan trees. Each tree was enclosed with rectangular-plan city wall (baluwerti or beteng), as shown in Yogyakarta and Surakarta today. What was actually the relationship between alun-alun and kedaton and candi in the Javanese tradition since the period of Singasari during the reign of Kartanegara (1222)? The emergence of alun-alun might have been earlier than Singasari’s alun-alun.

In Javanese oral tradition and several inscriptions found around Kediri and Malang, we find the emergence of the Javanese interpretation on Indic civilization, such as epic Mahabharata and statecraft negara; it was during the reign of Dharmawangsa (991–1016 AD). The king was also well known as the composer a state legal code called shivashasana, which is known in Bali as poerwadigama. The elaboration of Indic statecraft came into being under the reign of his son-in-law, Airlangga, who unified several Javanese kingdoms under his rule in Kahuripan from 1016 to 1042 AD.

Like his predecessor, Airlangga also commissioned the composition of the Javanese epic Arjunawiwaha. Both East Javanese rulers must also have interpreted Indic statecraft with mandala within the Javanese mancapat model, which is subtly preserved in gamelan music principle. Moreover, the change for the name of temple from caitya to candi could have occurred since the establishment of Kediri during the reign of Airlangga. The Javanese Mataram state under Dharmawangsa and Airlangga might be properly said as the period when the amalgam of Indic culture and indigenous Javanese tradition came into being.

Although activities related to alun-alun in Hindu-Buddhist Javanese tradition had not yet been described in detail, legends and traces of its relationship with kedaton/prabayeksa and candi were recognizable from court rituals and performances. In daily life, alun-alun must have remained empty because its spatiality and structure did not show any trace of public amusement and trade. The center of urbanism did not take place in the town square, but in the market (peken). Based on its current design, location, and position, alun-alun is a sacred square with two banyan trees at its center point that divide the axis of the kedaton symmetrically. The sacredness of alun-alun lies in its restricted use only for ceremonial purposes, instead of public use as a park or for amusement.

The other ancient trace of the origin of urbanism is religious cults and rituals demonstrating the affiliation between state and its citizens. As a politically organized entity, negara is the umbrella of its citizens. Symbolically, the banyan tree with its steadily sheltering structure at the center of the square represents the idea of shelter (pengayom) of people. Alun-alun has always been the stage where the rulers have an opportunity to show off their celestial performance and affiliation with clerics. Donating and worshipping must have been done by the rulers in alun-alun. In doing so, the benevolence and glory of the God represented by the ruler could have been demonstrated conspicuously. All this is made possible by state ceremony and ritual.

Although the concentration of populations occurred during the Singasari and Majapahit reigns, its already-urbanized sites of settlement did not leave us with any indication of insufficient resources. Singasari and Trowulan are located in a fertile plain with well-developed wet rice cultivation. In Javanese tradition, the disappearance of the political is conceived not simply as the dwindling light, but also as the relocation of the radiance of power to somewhere else. The former state capitals of Singasari and Majapahit remain empty and have been gradually demolished through time because there is no light anymore. It is, however, not the case for the former capital towns of Islamic Javanese states, for example Kotagede, Plered, Kartasura, Demak, Jepara, Banten, and Cirebon.

Identifying himself with the highest transcendence, the king must have been in constant need of validation of his lordly whim as the representation of the supreme being on earth. Airlangga in the Javanese tradition was well known as the first Javanese ruler who had successfully fulfilled his duty as king of cakravartin. Identifying themselves with the lord of mountain, Shiva and Wisnu for most Javanese Hindu kings could have been articulated with the royal title of Iswara, Narendra, Hyang, Wisnu, etc.

In dealing with the relationship between the importance of sawah and the sustenance of negara we necessarily trace back its origin from the court ritual and ceremony of offering gunungan (Daniels 2009, p. 25). The Javanese myth of the benevolence of rice, marriage, and fertility is in association with the Hindu goddess Dewi Sri, the wife of the god Wisnu. The model of cosmological mount, Mahameru made of rice decorated finely with meats, vegetables, and fruits is called tumpeng or gunungan, for Javanese people is not simply to articulate their thankfulness for their well-being. Rather, the tumpeng is the vivid reminder of the birth of cosmological wholeness that opens the world. The word gunungan is to articulate the prelude of the birth of cosmos. Traditionally, the figure of gunungan is used for the opening performance of puppet shadow plays (wayang kulit). Does offering a couple gunungan for the state ritual of grebeg have to do with the rebirth of the Javanese world? As a matter of fact the rebirth is a celebrating the occasion in which the relationship between the ruler and the people is demonstratively shown as the relation of the giver to the receiver.

The origin of gunungan, delivered by Yogyakarta and Surakarta courts, dates back to the myth of the Hindu sacred mount Mahameru, which is still believed by the Javanese people as the celestial image. Furthermore, the gunungan reminds people of the abode where their ancestors sit in their immortality. Beside its festivity, the state ritual and ceremony of offering large tumpeng or gunungan are likely the moments of truth for the Javanese people to realize their admiration and loyalty to the state as well as the world. The grebeg ceremony takes place every great day of Islam: the Ied Fitri and Haj or Ied Adha. During the ritual (upacara grebeg), the mound of rice (gunungan) is carried from the court to the mosque by passing through the public square (alun-alun), so that the cosmological relationship between religious center, mosque, and sociopolitical centre (kraton) comes into being for unity and oneness (manunggal).

The importance of rice cultivation has been incorporated in the traditional offering for any event and occasion articulating origin, initiation, commencement, and sustenance of oneness of home. Regardless of its transformation from Hindu-Buddhist to Islamic institution—from pura to mosque and from puri to kraton—the origin of grebeg might have been deeply rooted in ancient Javanese tradition associating the benevolence of rice for fertility, nourishment, and sustenance with the rebirth of state (negara) as well as that of cosmos.

Sultan Agung Hanyokrokusumo (1613–1645) was well known as the initiator and conductor of the grebeg ritual for the Javanese Mataram kingdom. He synthesized ancient indigenous pantheistic Animism, Hindu-Buddhist and Islamic tradition based on Javanese wisdom. Nevertheless, Sultan Agung was not the first king who enacted the dual role of state as the highest commander of the armed forces (senapati) and the highest religious leader and principal theologian (panatagama), but his grandfather, Panembahan Senapati, who founded the Islamic Javanese Mataram in Kotagede around 1575. Despite Islam forbidding any supremacy of metaphysics but God, the Javanese ruler still claims his metaphysical privilege as the best theologian.

Negara under Islamic Influence

Ideologically, Islam has not totally transformed the Javanese idea of power from the cult of the king to the abstract concept of supreme being. However, Islam has gradually diminished the centrality of the king’s metaphysical domination with Arabic literacy and its egalitarian doctrine on humanism. Islam was widely accepted by the people at coastal areas (pasisir) because their lives did not matter too much for the kingship and its despotic tradition. As mentioned earlier, Javanese court elites such as walisanga and other rulers—Senapati and Agung—acculturated Islamic teaching into Javanese culture, by means of their persuasive media of pewayangan. In doing so, the status quo was still in good shape in guarding their interest in the ruler cult.

The peaceful spread of Islamic influence through coastal traders to the hinterland of Java and Sumatra brought about the change and transformation of religious institutions. Islamic states came into being in the mostly coastal port towns, such as Pasai, Demak, Kudus, Jepara, Bantam, Jayakarta, Cirebon, Surabaya, and Makassar. Architecturally, the Islamic capital town was not very different from its Hindu-Buddhist predecessor. The difference was only the replacement of candi or pura by mosque. The trace of Hindu-Buddhist influence in its architecture is still apparent as shown in the Demak, Jepara, and Kudus mosque. Despite the fact that many modern mosques deploy dome form for their roofs, the use of Mount Meru-like forms for the roof is still a common tradition for the mosques in several Indonesian towns. The pile up parasol-pyramidal form of roof reflects the practice of syncretism in architecture.

The concentration of Muslim settlement at the traditional Javanese town center has taken place surrounding the town mosque since the end of the sixteenth century. Since then the Muslim quarter, called kauman, has become an integrated part of the Javanese town structure and identity. Occupied mostly by Arabian, Gujarati, and native traders, kauman is the domain with its own features, as an enclave for learning Islamic teachings and reciting Koran. Traders from this Islamic domain have enacted as peddlers between the hinterland and coastal vicinities for specific commodities: gold, salt, spices, silk, beads, pearls, etc.

It is presumably not the town and its palace the center of negara, but the public open space for grand ritual and ceremony called alun-alun. However, the question remains as to whether Javanese ancient kingdoms are states without towns because of simply technical reason or ideological priority. As a matter of fact, the emergence of Hindu and Buddhist temples in Java since the eighth century gives us the indication that negara as a sociopolitically organized community is able to build and establish large scale monumental edifice. Why did they not build their town? It has been suggested that the artefacts of house, town, and country for Indonesian people must have been simply conceived as the stage (Javanese kelir) to perform the episode of nation spectacle (lakon).

Tearing down historical buildings in post-colonial Indonesia occurred during the reigns of Sukarno and Suharto for the sake of politically updating assertion. On the other hand, erecting new monuments for asserting the new spirit of reign epoch became mandatory. Both modern leaders used the same method in accusing their opponents of being anti-state and its ideology.

The sense of belonging among Indonesians does not lie simply in its geopolitical map of history, but deeply rooted in its spiritual origin as the carriers and sustainers of the concept of Malay-ness and Java-ness. Accordingly, dwelling and sitting are bound up with the necessity for being able to bring everything toward oneness through syncretism. Then the rises and falls of negara are likely the drama of the reigns itself in which the state as a sociopolitically organized entity mobilizes people to perform their theater of the ruler cult. The Javanese state shares in large with Balinese negara in the nineteenth century on this intentionality, as observed meticulously by Clifford Geertz (1980).

Historically, kuta negara in the Javanese tradition is conceptually sustainable, from Singasari, Majapahit, Demak, Kotagede, and Plered to Yogyakarta and Surakarta. Although the changes of name and form occur through time, for instance the replacement of pura with mesjid agung (grand mosque), the basic concept of khita as the political, cultural, and spiritual center of negara remains the same. At its very core is the emptiness of alun-alun guarded by twin banyan trees. Close to the emptiness are the seat of ruler and the place of spiritual congregation. Alun-alun, kraton, and mesjid are architecturally identical to inner rooms of the Javanese house: sentong tengah, sentong kiwa, and sentong tengen.

The identity of house and town is articulated in the multilayered dalem, literally meaning inner, inside, home, and unified realm. The most inner domain is the inner self (batin) of man in which the unity of spirit, mind, and body finds its being. Dalem is spatiotemporally multilayered in the way of embracing inner and outer self within oneness. In the old Malay tradition of Deli Serdang, the idea of house, town, and country is conceptually composed of the concept of jeru with similar meaning with the Javanese dalem. Hence the identity of house, town, and country is not simply symbolic in the way of projecting the celestial realm on earth. Rather, the identity of domains lies in the concept of home in conceptually multilayered realms of relationship between human beings and their living place.

Understanding the concept of center in Javanese thought, which has predominantly occupied the Indonesian polity and culture, must not have overlooked the importance of dalem, which is in its broadest sense the sacred domain of inspiring silence (nglangut) and stillness (hening). Letting the silence speak to us by meditation has been traditionally practiced as the way the Javanese people come up to determination. Architecturally, the sacred domain of town such as Yogyakarta is neither in the mosque nor in the court or palace, but in its town square. This is the place where stillness and silence are. Being guarded by the twin banyan trees, alun-alun keeps its secrecy in itself.

From his highest place (siti hinggil), the ruler sits and meditates by gazing through the space of alun-alun between the twin banyan trees and so forth to the mountain and sky, and lets from there the stillness and silence speak to him. On the highest place there is a chamber within a chamber. The innermost chamber (bangsal manguntur tangkil) is actually the place where the ruler sits for his silence and stillness, whereas its outer chamber is the chamber of the origin of being (bangsal witana). In daily life, the chambers remain empty. From this emptiness, the idea of center is derived symbolically with an architecturally modest structure.

As the capital of Mataram Sultanate, Yogyakarta is also one of the most important centers of the Islamic Javanese state. The Javanese Mataram Kingdom was in decline after Sultan Agung’s death in 1630. After decades of turmoil and power struggle in the court elites, Prince Mangkubumi Sukowati had been given a concession to build a new kingdom based on the Giyanti agreement. Yogyakarta as the capital of the Javanese kingdom was founded after the Giyanti treaty in 1755. According to the pact, the Mataram Kingdom was divided into two rulers: Prince Mangkubumi in the west and Pakubuwono I in the east.

The history of Yogyakarta has been depicted with metaphors and symbolic elements that are incorporated in buildings, landscape elements, and ornaments; this symbolism is manifest in a similar way to the Balinese context (Geertz 1980) because both share the same root in Animism and Hindu-Buddhism. The site clearing for the seat of his kingdom was begun in 1756. Mangkubumi himself led the site selection and opened the site for his kraton. The first thing he did was to find land between two rivers with a strong axial direction between mountain and sea. Balance of right and left and strong front connection to mountain are important considerations for his seat. In fact, the location of the kraton is perfect on the plain between the river Winanga and Code.

The center of the site was determined with a view to Mount Merapi in the north and south plateaus on the south. The importance of the ruler’s seat is to determine the highest area called siti hinggil (highest land). From this area the ruler as architect defines the site for the palace that belongs to the innermost negara called kadaton. In Yogyakarta, there are two highest sites situated on the north and on the south along the north-south axis. The area of kedaton is located to include both highest sites into a continuous enclosure. The north area is dedicated to be the main palace while the south is for a secondary royal enclosure. The function of the main palace is not only for the royal residence, but also as the ceremonial platform of royal performance such as grebeg.

Site clearing was made to determine the open area for three main domains: the mosque area (masjid hageng), the square (alun-alun), and the palace (kedaton). The three essential components must be well established with a definite boundary. The square was marked out with the row of banyan trees while the areas for the palace and the mosque were delineated with the foundation of the wall. Similar layers of domain are applied for the mosque and palace complex. They have principally three layers—purwa (elementary), madya (intermediate), and hutama (primary)—that correspond to the depth of realm. The innermost area is characterized with the most intense category of meeting and the destination or the place of stop. The elementary domain is actually the reception area of all visitors, whereas the second and the third domain are the refinement of procession toward the goal. The interface of the palace and the temple area is the square alun-alun.

From the highest site, the axial line of development was drawn to the north and to the south. In doing so, the view to Mount Merapi is clear from the highest site at the north side. On the south, the highest land has a view to the southern mountainous lands. At the middle part of the north and the south highest site there is an imaginary axis denoting the east-west direction.

The development was continued to establish the square (alun-alun) on the south and on the north. The twin banyan trees were planted at the center area of the north and south square. On the north, the intersection of the center line of the palace and the center line of the mosque is the area between the twin banyan trees. There is an open question whether the twin banyans are to replace the twin dvarapala. The installment of twin banyans is to guard the kraton. This case is commonly found in most Buddhist temples (Malalasekera 1990, p. 53). The twin guardian figures stand with fierce face protecting the inner space of the temple. Kraton or dalem in this case is conceived as temple complex. Twin banyan trees are not only installed on the south of the palace, but they are also to guard the kraton on the north. The banyan trees stand within the square at the north and south as spiritual guards of the innermost part of kuta negara.

The innermost area is actually the zone where the king and his family reside. Inside the palace there are four zones or complexed: siti hinggil (elevated area), kemandungan (engendering area), kemagangan (candidature/apprentice area), and jengkar dalem. The four zones have their specific functions and symbolic meanings. Each zone is provided with halls (bangsal) and buildings (gedong) for their ceremonial and functional purposes. The connection between the zones is architecturally signified with a gate (regol). The king’s home is the jengkar dalem, literally meaning inner self. The whole zone of the jengkar dalem and its buildings is called kedaton.

Landlord System and Taxation

Economically, the Javanese kingdom depends on land cultivation and its tax. Land ownership status is given by the king to his families or noblemen (praja/priyayi) outside kraton; this tax on farming settlements is called lungguh (Onghokam 2003, p. 172; Headley 2004, p. 164; Schriecke 1955). Inside the palace wall, the land is directly controlled by the king and is known as siti daleman. It is the land for the king’s residence and his ceremonial supporters, from musicians to soldiers. The land is well known as bumi narawita, literally meaning earth for the king’s existence. Units of land under management of noblemen are usually used for their seats. The land of lungguh is actually the king’s direct land territory (negara agung), which is represented by and lent to his men or relatives with status of anggaduh, literally meaning pretending to be owned. In return, they are due to pay tax and tribute on a periodical basis. Paying tribute is traditionally made with visit of loyalty to the king for state rituals and ceremonies.

Most lands of lungguh are subject to be taken back by the king in cases where the landlords are not on duty as state officers anymore. However, in many cases the lands are able to be borrowed for generations as long as the property taxes and tributes are paid to the king. The distance of their relation to the king determines the area of their land of lungguh. The prime minister (patih) gets the largest land of lungguh, followed by other ministers and regents. As mentioned earlier, the lands of lungguh are the seats of the landlord, but if it is not the case and located outside the capital town or in rural area, the land is entrusted to somebody called bekel. Under his management, the bekel cultivates the land for agriculture (sawah, tegalan) or rents the land for other purposes, such as for shops, houses, or other productive facilities. In order to cultivate the land, the bekel hires commoner/peasants as workers called nara karya, who are usually granted to live with their families in the land of lungguh on a rental basis. Then the land of lungguh is divided into several parts or units called cacah. Each cacah is cultivated by a group of peasants consisting of five to twenty-five households. The head of cacah reports to the bekel for the production and cultivation of the land.

The function of the bekel is to utilize the land as productively as possible so that the bekel is able to pay property tax to his landlord. Depending on the area, which is measured in units of jung and karya—1.428 Ha—the land of lunguh is subject to taxation or tribute. The collector of the tax, called patuh, is actually a state officer (Miyazaki 1988, p. 30; Nakamura 2012, p. 21) who works for the king or noblemen. His job is to collect the tax for the king and for the landlords according to a certain proportion, which is determined by the state. Of course, at the bottom of the taxation are the peasants who do not have the right of land. They have to work for their bekel and pay cultivation tax for their patuh. Besides, they have to work voluntarily to maintain the roads (marga desa), dam and irrigation systems (bendungan), bridges (kreteg), act as community night guards (ronda), and in case of emergency (gugur gunung).

In rural areas, the bekel is the extension of the king’s hand and head of community settlement called desa lungguh. As a socioeconomic and political institution, desa lungguh is a miniature state in a rural environment with the bekel as the top political position. He is assisted by persons in charge of specific duties. However, the political structure of desa is less hierarchical and more egalitarian than kuta negara or kuta praja. Economically, desa lungguh is an agricultural settlement based on sawah cultivation. Indeed, desa lungguh is not a traditional Javanese wanua, which is originally a village republic with a self-contained socioeconomic system. However, they share a similar function as an agricultural community settlement. Traditionally, as an agricultural community a Javanese desa is provided with persons in charge of public safety and order (tuha wanua), irrigation (huluair), season and weather (wariga), customs and traditions (pinuntun), barn and granary (makalangkang), forest (tuhalas), and public health (hulu wras).

In the urban context, city blocks are actually a system of domains called lungguhan, which are divided by roads (marga negara). The main road (marga hutama) acts as the access road to the city square (alun-alun). Prior to Yogyakarta, a long axial road had not been implemented. Previous Javanese towns did not have such spatial axes. The main function of roads is mostly economic in connecting the capital city to the domains of lungguh, from rural and remote areas to capital towns (kuta praja) and cities (kuta negara). Since 1830, the Dutch colonial rule forced a cultivation system in Java. From then until 1850, most villagers either from desa wanua or from desa lungguh had to pay taxed and commodities to the colonial regime in addition to their routine tax (pajeg) and tribute (pupundhutan) to their local ruler.

State Ceremony

The palace as a whole does not show something more special than its ritual and ceremonial occasions such as grebeg. The ceremony takes place three times per year: the birthday of the Prophet Muhammad (grebeg maulud), the Ied Fitri (grebeg sawal), and the Ied Adha (grebeg besar). The king and his family will sit in the hall of ponconity in his kedaton, waiting for the commencement of the grebeg. When everything is ready, he moves to the hall of witana (origin, source, heavenly seat) in the siti hinggil zone. It is this zone where the people pay homage to their ruler. During the grebeg ceremony, all halls and buildings are lively, occupied by various activities. Preparing foods for communal meals takes place in the kamadungan zone. It is the zone where the central kitchens are located: gebulen in the west and sekulagen in the east. Both symbolically represent the engendering power of center from birth to death.

The activities in the zone of kamagangan are dominated by youth members of the royal family and royal officers. Colorful costumes and attire are part of their dance and ceremonial performances. They represent the emerging generation of the center that sustains negara’s glory and splendor. The ritual and ceremony of grebeg gathers all royal components from every zoning group inside and outside the palace wall (beteng). Ceremonially, the conception of grebeg ceremony had been introduced by Sunan Kalijaga and other members of Wali Sanga in various places of Islamic kingdoms such as Demak, Kudus, Surabaya, and Jepara in the sixteenth century. The Demak’s grebeg besar has been commemorated as the inauguration of Raden Patah (1478–1518) as the first sultan of the Demak kingdom in 1503. However, the grand performance of grebeg ceremony was initiated and composed by Sultan Agung Hanyokrokusumo of the Mataram Sultanate (1613–1645); the ceremony and festival of gerebeg had been used by Sultan Agung to integrate the new year of the Islamic calendar system with the Javanese almanac.

Negara in the Indonesian and Javanese context is appropriately understood as a cosmological concept instead of merely a secular notion of political institution. Negara or negari is the source of the cosmic order. It is the destination of paying homage and respect demonstrated with the use of highly courteous manners and language. Urbanity in the Javanese sense is indispensable from the rituals and ceremonies associated with the ruler, court (kraton or istana), and negara. It has nothing to do directly with the concentration of settlement and public service institutions. Most of institutions in the context of negara are for state ritual and ceremonies. The central purpose of negara is to perform a splendor occasion that invokes and revalidates the cosmological unity of dualistic antagonism at various levels and forms of existence. Indeed, the structure of negara is for day-to-day state business, but its entire course of business is to anticipate and celebrate the rebirth of the cosmos called grebeg or keduri.

The history of Javanese rulers from Kahuripan’s Airlangga, Majapahit’s Raden Wijaya, Demak’s Jaka Tingkir, Singasari’s Ken Arok, Mataram’s Panembahan Senapati and Agung Hanyokrokusumo, to the modern Indonesia Sukarno and Suharto, told us the importance of the one who was able to bring back the world in order. It is, in most cases, neither the question of legitimacy nor legal validity. Rather, the successful candidate for ruler must be somebody with strong willpower or a renegade. A smoothly sailing candidate is a seemingly weak leader for the Javanese ideal.

Although sometimes brutal, cruel, and merciless, a savior, liberator, or restorer is more favorable than a hereditary successor. The Javanese saying jer basuki mawa beya confirms that everything worthy and better comes after overcoming the danger or crisis. The saviors and heroes of the Javanese world will not be judged on their past biographical data before they come to power. What is important is the acquiring and winning moment to lead toward successful restoration of the cosmic order. The myth of his cult begins from those moments. The moment of the proclamation of independence of the Republic of Indonesia was the myth for Sukarno as well as the moment of dubious Supersemar (presidential instruction on March 11, 1967) for Suharto. Javanese rulers struggle to monumentalize such moments and try to convince themselves by building prasasti (monumental inscriptions) and tugu (memorial statues). Suharto built his monuments in commemoration of his early achievements in Yogyakarta: Yogya Kembali (1985) and Serangan 1 Maret (1974). In doing so, Suharto would have inserted his presence as a national hero and savior of nation from early on in his career in the Indonesian Military and political system.

Modern Kota Negara and the Myth of the Past

Kota is an Indonesian word for the Javanese kuta. Kota in general is a political institution of settlement with certain status. Past buildings and places are subject to the validation of their possibly adjusted relationship and commitment with the contemporary ruler. It is not surprising to witness the fact that the change of their majors, walikota, means the possible transformation of its symbolic center, main boulevards, seat of authority and institutions, alun-alun, mosques, pasar, etc., whereas the citizens, warga, are not the passive spectators of the state event. Rather, town dwellers are spectators and performers at once who participate in the making of the spectacle. Nationwide, as mentioned earlier, the celebration of Indonesian Independence Day on August 17 becomes more spiritual than political.

The state occasion is considerable the vivid spectacle when the concept of home within the framework of negara is demonstratively performed in various enjoyable gatherings (kenduri). After all, if the ruler is not able to maintain semangat because of fatigue and exhaustion, it is also the time when chaos, anarchy, turmoil, and disorder come into being. Then a new ruler is waiting to pick up a new semangat for signifying the call for the rebirth of cosmos. Every epoch (jaman) must likely have had its own ruler (penguasanya) and its own semangat. And every ruler must have had its own stage with its own story and performance.

As mentioned earlier, Javanese people, however modern, believe in ancestral bless (restu leluhur), which has been molded into various religious attitudes and conducts. What lies beneath all efforts is the semangat, which is considerably manifested in various forms and names but one. Sukarno spoke of semangat in the context of liberation from colonialism and Indonesian nation building, whereas Suharto asserted semangat for national development. Thematically, it is the semangat that is persistently retrieved to bring about people into oneness. The rulers are the conductors for making the semangat manifest into actions and concerts. Since towns and cities must likely have been the stage for the rulers to perform their semangat, their architectural expressions and characteristics must have been embedded in the epochs associated with those in reign.

Among coastal people in the archipelago, semangat has been ritually picked up from the ocean with the ceremony of jamu laut (entertaining the sea). In Javanese tradition, semangat has been conceived as isi in terms of kesekten (contain, essence, and charisma). The articulation of semangat in the daily life of towns and cities becomes a spiritually motivating credo for reminding citizens of their identity (Forschee 2006, p. 85). Being unified in a semangat enables the rulers to conduct their spectacles. All architectural efforts and resources must have mobilized within the framework of semangat to make its events.

Monumental buildings, skyscrapers, and upscale facilities are the vehicle (wahana) of semangat for validating the charm of rulers in various state events. Sukarno and Suharto have built their charms as pemimpin besar revolusi (great leader of revolution) and bapak pembangunan (father of development). As done by the past rulers, the first and second presidents of Indonesia had entitled themselves with their own semangat that were conceived as their enlightenment. Then the image of towns and cities of Indonesia might have not simply been associated with its historical collage of buildings and parks. Rather, the urban image here is inseparable from the present semangat incorporated by its present rulers in the built environment. Accordingly, updating to the contemporary in most cases must be more preferable than holding and maintaining its memorial contents of the past.

Change and transformation of the built environment are likely not the subject of formally legitimating system. Rather, in Indonesia they are the subject of relevance and harmonization with the semangat held by the ruler in reign. The incorporation of semangat in daily polity is in the form of ideology. Despite their various versions, the Indonesian rulers from Sukarno to Susilo Bambang Yudhoyono have exercised their semangat within the framework of an ascetically creed of state philosophy (pancasila). Even though after the fall of Suharto, rational movements of political groups for the rectification of state constitution of 1945 have been made, there is likely nothing that will change with its state ideology (pancasila). The recent development in 2003 of the Indonesian polity shows us even more convincing evidence of the preservation of constitution and state ideology with the newly established state institution called mahkamah konstitusi (the constitution court).

Although power in Javanese or Indonesian culture is not an abstract formulation (Anderson 2007, p. 5), mystery and secrecy belong to its foundation. The purpose is clear, which is deeply rooted in the tradition of the ruler cult. To establish a center is, however, not merely to institute a settlement for concentration of populations as well as power. Rather, founding a center is to set up the place of happening where the ruler is able to mobilize people going to alun-alun. Establishing a center is neither the question of legitimacy nor authority. The question of ability to rule is the capacity for mobilizing people for his/her own sake: the self-cult. The problem of rulers in Indonesia seems not simply the competition for best idea or program. Rather, rulers used to have a single thought (semangat) that unifies people with charismatic appeal. Rhetoric and subtle cruelty are historically part of the method of this intentionality. Without having these capacities, they will be quickly forgotten without substantial and memorable legacy.

Concluding Remarks

In order to understand the Javanese town and state, it is necessary to discuss the concept of kuta negara in the context of Javanese cosmology. Accordingly, power is perceived as the light endowed by the ruler in the form of personal charisma and spiritual reverence. The light in this sense is not only incorporated in the leadership capacity of the ruler, but also endowed as courage and persistence in achieving the successful achievement. The powerful achievement is not about right or wrong in terms of justice, legitimacy, and law. It is properly said about ability to restore order and peace. A strong ruler is the one who is able to rise to the occasion for restoring the world from chaos and disintegration.

The relation of power to territoriality is analogically like the source and its light on the ground. The brighter and stronger the light is, the larger and wider the influence of its power. The flexibility of light is not simply expansionist for physical and political domination, but it is more or less spiritual. First of all, it is about the idea of a bulb to bring everything under its light. In contrast to the Western idea of space and territory that is measurable and accountable as well as tangibly referential, the Javanese idea of domain and boundary is somewhat spatially symbolic and flexible. Territory in the Javanese sense is not a matter of area demarcation with a physically fixed boundary. It is rather a conceptually elastic area of spiritual and cosmic influence of the ruler. Charisma and political charm as well as spiritual appeal play very important roles in the concept of spatial influence. Since the intentionality of negara is the grand spectacle of power, building the city as a permanent structure is likely not in the interest of the ruler cult. Permanent structure does not support the flexible need of grandeur performance once in a lifetime. In other words, Javanese towns and cities are not built for their citizens but for their rulers. And the rulers understand cities and towns are nothing but their stage for their theater of power.

Chapter Four

Javanese Way of Living

What is living in Javanese culture? This chapter attempts to search for meanings of ordinary life in the Javanese tradition. The chapter was carried out from the author’s personal life experience as a native Javanese and literary studies on Javanese culture and tradition. The focus of the chapter is to understand the relationship between the concepts and phenomena of the living world in daily life. The chapter will unfold and examine local concepts to find their relation to other concepts, that is, to unveil and dismantle the Javanese values and significances for their sense of home. In doing so, cultural locality is not to be just the site of happenings, but also the structure and resources that determine and work out the way of living.

Living as Walking

Linguistically, the region of Javanese dwelling culture used to be situated in Central and East Java. However, today the realm of Javanese culture has, for the most part, established Indonesian lifestyle. Javanese customs are considered predominant not merely because of their overriding populations spread out in the archipelago, but also because Javanese people occupy most of the key positions in Indonesian polity from local, regional, to national bureaucracy. Regarding its geopolitical context, the Javanese dwelling tradition and culture must have been established for the most part in the Indonesian way of life. Likewise, it would not be an exaggeration to say that Javanese influence in Indonesian living tradition is stipulated with its culturally assimilating leadership in shaping and establishing a national cultural identity.

What is the Javanese way of living? The best entry point for studying the Javanese way of life is probably from the idea and performance of the Javanese shadow puppet plays, wayang. Most Javanese people must have known and experienced wayang performance as part of their cultural education because it is considered their living philosophy and religion. Regarding this strategic importance, wayang becomes the best medium of power relation. Most religions—Hinduism, Buddhism, Christianity, and Islam—and ideologies—Liberalism, Marxist-Communist, Socialism, and Pancasilasm—in Java have never utilized wayang as their ideological propaganda medium (McVey 1986; Taylor et al. 1991, p. 22). What is important in the wayang performance is not simply storytelling because the date and procedure of the occasion have to fulfill certain rituals and feasts. To a certain extent, the preparation of the performance is not simply technical, but also full of mystical aspects, which are sophisticated and mysterious. Moreover, the most valuable of the performance is its capacity as the spiritual messenger of the Javanese ideals for ethics and aesthetics. Regarding this capacity, political leaders and their ideological machinery try to use wayang performance for their political interest and agenda.

There is nothing more important in their lives than being a Javanese. Being Javanese is to articulate the quality of behavior of satria in wayang performance, which is behaviorally perfect in terms of kindness, fineness, courteousness, politeness, hospitability, cordiality, gentleness, and respectfulness. The ideals of satria are, of course, more than that because it is a figure and model of leader with knightly self-disciplined manners and an ideologically convincing personality. The five pandawa knights in the Epics of Mahabarata are probably the most representative figures of Javanese satria. Indeed, the figure of satria is not always a perfect and handsome person like Arjuna or Gatotkaca. Hanuman, Subali, Sugriwa, and Srikandi are also considered satria, though their physical properties do not belong to a male human. What is important to emphasize in the Javanese culture is that satria is a halus (cultivated) person with the capacity of a hero. In the minds of Javanese, being halus is a sign of power that requires the acts and trainings for the concentration of energy (Anderson 2007, p. 39). The satria’s culture and tradition are well known by the Javanese people in terms of priyayi’s milieu or gentry. The priyayi people set apart from the rest of Javanese society because of their traditional privileges in terms of education, power relation, and social standing in the state bureaucracy.

Even though the majority of Javanese society is not the priyayi people, this elite group plays an important role in shaping and developing the system of values and norms in almost all aspects of the Javanese world, from economy and politics to sociocultural life. The most classical part of the performance of wayang depicts the Javanese priyayi’s world. However, the voice of ordinary people (wong cilik, or rakyat), plays an important segment in the whole performance when the servants, punakawan Semar, Petruk, Gareng, and Bagong, have the words to say. The performance tries to portray the mythological messages of the priyayi and the contemporary issues in a continuous storyline. However, the whole idea of the wayang story and performance is inseparable from the necessity for depicting life as a story of walking (lakon).

Lakon in its conceptual capacity is the story of life that transforms the Javanese world from chaos to orderly system. The word lakon is derived from laku meaning walking, behaving, and going on stride. Despite its chaotic and devastating beginning, the Javanese life is a story of struggle for walking the line that transforms rudeness and impertinence to fineness and greatness.

Place in the Javanese tradition is grasped as the characteristic site where events and daily activities take place. The Javanese place is meaningless without any relation to laku (walk, behavior, action, and role of actors) or lakon (plot, scenario, story, and performance). As any performance, the significance of place is always associated with the lakon enacted. As shown in the shadow puppet play (wayang kulit), the idea of place is inseparable from the events and its role maker, not the edifice of the place. Building and dwelling are nothing but nglakoni as well as living. Nglakoni is derived from the words laku and lakon. How to build and to dwell is associated with the necessity for going for a walk and walking through time.

The other meanings of lakon are experiencing a journey or trip; life is a sojourn for having a drink (urip mung mampir ngombe). The question is: What is actually the destination of such journey (lakon)? How is it possible to find home if living means walking through time? Since in Javanese tradition the purpose of living is never more clearly formulated than walking through time, it must have been suggested to consider whiling away as the essence of dwelling. How can we enjoy the time if we do not understand our being?

The question concerning being (ana) has always been understood by the Javanese tradition in the context of experiencing oneness (manunggal). Accordingly, the aim of living is not to establish a permanent place, but it is essentially to enable people to experience oneness at any level of representation and meaning. After all this, the Javanese people call such an achievement kelakon, meaning having been experienced and gone through. What lies behind the concept of kelakon? Regarding dwelling is a walking through. Kelakon is the light at the end of the tunnel of living that is actually being on the safe track. The destination itself is oneness, not to speak of individual profile and achievement. In other words, there is no place for an egoistically established cult in Javanese tradition but the ruler. The concept of ruler itself is fundamentally established as the center of dwelling that brings about the existing oneness to grow. Needless to say the impact of the cult of center has led the Javanese people to immortalize their rulers, instead of commemorating their being as well as walking on the safe track.

Given that, the Javanese people perceive building and dwelling in the sense of means for being on the move in terms of walking, is the meaning of the built environment they build simply a vehicle of moving forward? Walking as a matter of fact is the act of moving on rhythmic stride. The sense of Javanese walking (mlaku) lies in the steady and consistent pace; it is a movement without hesitation and hurry, but simply doing the move joyfully and constantly. In its essence, mlaku is experiencing the moments with continuous acceptance of the circumstances (sumarah) without worry and ready to deal with any situation with patience (sabra) and perseverance (tawakal). All these concepts are derived from Arabic words, but the content is a philosophically Javanese Hindu-Buddhist way of transcending ego into emptiness in terms of anatman.

Regarding life as walking, the Javanese-built environment could be seen as the stage of the performance of going together in various styles, manners, scales, forms, and events. Since lakon also means character of the play, living as nglakoni is considered enacting to serve for collective oneness. Being is actually designed at the service of ritual events and ceremonies, home place in Javanese tradition would have been meaningless without its ritual feast (slametan). It is the reason why in daily life, the Javanese-built environment shows itself as an empty place. All domains pertain to the designated places during the performance of slametan.

As mentioned earlier, Javanese are taught by their traditions from early childhood to be able to manage their emotions and feelings for sabar (patient) and prihatin (concern). All these are believed to prepare them for being able to survive in any bad time by meditation (semedi) and reflection (tawekal). Architecturally, eling and prihatin are incorporated in the building expression as modest appearance.

At any level of expression, the Javanese way to use form and color are aptly with mattw and earthy. All this represents and reflects the Javanese preference of harmony with earth and nature accordingly. There is no need for being obvious and self-important. The Javanese culture is likely repressive for expression and profile on an individual basis. The change and destruction of the built environment will not be able to hurt the Javanese feeling because they do not identify their life and being in such artifacts. It does not mean that Javanese do not care for the collectively memorized experience with things.

The Javanese people are traditionally trained to not be sentimental in the way of being able to self-control based on the belief that time will resolve their problems and that life must go on in the sense of legawa (generous and altruistic). Avoiding open conflicts and confrontations are the attitude of the Javanese in dealing with any problem and change. They believe in syncretism at any rate of cultural phenomena: from ideas to concrete things.

The way of syncretism is facilitated by the mechanism of nrima in a positive way. It implies the readiness to accommodate all possibilities and to manage the problems without any prejudice but careful listening. As long as the change is introduced carefully and persuasively they will listen. All this is not by the grace and blessings of Javanese culture, but it lies in the nature of human existence that he/she is always able to live on the earth with the capacity of learning.

The sense of home for Javanese is never out of learning to be fit (cocok) with their feelings and emotions. Clifford Geertz describes the concept of cocok comprehensively in terms of a somewhat harmoniously experienced (Geertz 1973, pp. 129–30). It is, however, an effortless process that one feels at ease with his/her environment. Learning in the search for the sense of home is perceived by the Javanese sense of nglakoni: the concept of the ability to live and to deal with any problem and challenge without fear and anxiety.

Nglakoni is learning to be open-minded and is based on the belief that any case and circumstance has its own lesson, wisdom, and message. The Javanese are taught by their traditions to manage and control their behavior to be a priyayi: to be a civilized humankind with full respect to others and able to control their instinct and desire. It is the reason why the change of their environment at any rate does not create panics and restlessness, because they believe in the nature of the things that there are no permanent circumstances but the change itself. All this is conceived by the Javanese in the idea of sumeleh.

The integration of the built environment in its context and setting is conceived in the idea of nglaras: to find the way of harmonious togetherness or uniting behind. The presence of building work in this sense is not to make any high-profile expression. The Javanese tradition rejects with its classical credo, ojo dumeh, any attempt or way of expression that is apt to show personal opulence and less concern about harmony. Apparently disregarding its relation to others is considered not a Javanese way of expression.

Establishing place of dwelling, therefore, is not merely to disclose the location with its surrounding and society, but also to contribute to the presence of rahayu, meaning well-being, beauty, healthy, and cultivation. In this context, the place of dwelling must have been developed within the ecosystemic totality in which every element or being has its own respective and genuine contribution to the hurip (dynamic living system) for cultural refinement (alus). Being cultivated, alus is conceived as the cultural process to put all potential sources and materials together into a harmonious composition. This is what in the Javanese tradition is called nglaras. The outcome of the belief that everything is principally able to find its way and contribute to being in wholeness might have led the Javanese culture toward its cultural imperialism.

Moment and Home

The Javanese culture avoids going into a self-grandeur expression for the place of dwelling except for state-related events and ceremonies. The Javanese people believe that every event has its wanci, its own moment, for demonstrating the idea of oneness. In its revelation, wanci is non-linear temporality, but cyclically repetitive. Wanci is not a temporal scheme based on mathematical measurement in which a convention of actions in space is plotted in a measurable system. Wanci is only an entry point of events and happenings in a cyclic repetition that is to reinstate the oneness of the world and its orderly system.

The ritual events and happenings, slametan and ruwatan, reinstatement, and other religious celebrations, are the occasions when cosmic powers show themselves to recover the crises in the world. It is the way the Javanese preserve and conserve the oneness of their world. The call for restoring and recovering the oneness is given with crises in which crises come into being. The crises not only pertain to transition and change, but also to articulating the common care in the sense of the poetical mission of humankind: memayu ayuning bawana (care for beautifying the world).

The call for saving the world is the time when the necessity for the center is demonstrated. Family lifecycle rituals are the time to establish center at the house. Traditionally, a Javanese family has to set up their own center that is made possible with kaprajan (a respective state service or other public servant). Regardless of its ranking and position, state or public service in Javanese culture is highly regarded as holding a respective seat in the society. House type and roof style are traditionally determined by the owner’s seat in Javanese society. Although merchants and traders can build their houses, restricted form and type may be applied respectively.

Although in the Javanese language the work of art is associated with the concept of reka articulating (creative work, figure, painting, artificial, and mind), its context and application are restricted as the medium of event for demonstrating the idea of oneness. Erecting a settlement or a house is never associated with the concept of reka because such works have already been established with typological form (pakem). In some way the concept of reka sounds deviant, out of conformity, and counterfeit. It is, however, not to say that creativity and innovation do not have special room in the Javanese tradition. The affirmative account for creativity is mbangun, a synonym of reka. Mbangun is actually building, erecting, establishing, sustaining, and developing. Its context, however, is not only to establish the structure and shelter, but also to renew and recover the earth. Creative work in the sense of mbangun has always emphasized its constructive intention for making the world whole and beautiful.

The message of mbangun is to cultivate the land and to make a free area for possible construction. Regarding the built environment as the work of art, its composition might have been established with sensible technique and treatment. Since the concept of art in Javanese culture does not manifest at the level of abstract formulation, it is difficult to grasp the work under a single artistic concept without taking its various Javanese ideas of beauty.

Beauty related to the built environment in the Javanese tradition comprises various concepts: rahayu, apik, endah, and lestari. However, the ancient Javanese concept of beauty concerning building was more comprehensive in its formulation under the notion of hita. In the old Javanese language, every building has its essentially designated beauty called hita concerning its relationship with human being. Nonetheless, hita is not simply somewhat functional or utilitarian. Rather, hita is beauty because of its capacity for giving and delivers an inventively assenting thing for human well-being.

The sense of beauty in the framework of hita is expressed in the word Hlam-hlam, meaning kind and beneficial. Since hita is a Sankrit loanword literally meaning well-being, its synonym in Javanese language is rahayu, which is widely and commonly used with the word sugeng (safe, vigorous, vital, and healthy). Thus, sugeng rahayu is the concept for the state of being safe, fit, fine, well, and living in perfect shape. Generally the concept is to emphasize dwelling as walking through on the safe track.

Although carpentry and millwork is a longstanding profession for Javanese traditional building construction, there is no indication that wooden architecture finds its way for free expressions. Design in the Javanese way is commonly understood within the scheme of pakem (pattern, scheme, and format). Living house must have been reproduced generation to generation based on its already-established model (pakem). Although traditional master builders (hundagi) have disappearing for several centuries, their pakem is still alive. The application of pakem in the built environment is to establish the stately ordering system to acknowledge and to affirm the social status of its owners.

Although the scheme of composition and construction has been already formulated with pakem, it does not mean that the Javanese do not have any respect and honor to innovative artistic works. The space for innovation is still on hand within the agenda of making events. Then the problem of artistic work in Javanese culture does not lie in the exploration of form and style, but concerns the capacity of the work as stage of the world. The utmost endeavor for works of art is to incorporate the rituals and ceremonies that sustain and develop the world toward its beautiful oneness. Architecturally, the Javanese house is to reinstate its capacity as the theater of family rituals.

Family rituals in the Javanese tradition are the occasions when the sense of oneness is experienced. Being guided with rituals, dalem shows itself as the pedestal of community. Ancient traces of Pantheistic Animism are demonstrated with solemn mantra reciting verses from Al Qur’an. The purpose of gathering in the house during family ritual is to reinstate their wish and hope for being on the safe track of oneness.

Slametan as a concept stems from the word slamet, meaning safe and secure. The event of slametan is never in perfect shape without performing the mound of rice (tumpeng) as the center of the feast. The form of tumpeng itself is to recall the image of the tree of life (kapaltaru) and the statue of the mountain of heaven (Mahameru). As in ancient times, the conductor of the ceremony is a cleric (modin or kiayi) and the keynote speaker is the most respected person in the community. In general, the affiliation of religious and sociopolitical leadership always comes into terms with the redefinition and reinstatement of oneness.

The Javanese sustainable relationship between building and dwelling is comprehended with the concept of open, diopeni and rawat, dirawati. The words imply the care for its service to humankind. The ideas of open and rawat are the way of keeping the things’ presence and at hand for human being. It is not simply seen as a functional relationship between subject and object.

House as the place of dwelling seems to have been integrated as the extension of body in terms of container (wadah). The relationship constitutes the sense of pomah (being at home) because of the care for its service. Ritual offerings (sajen) are delivered by the owner to the ancestors and spirits that are believed to guard the house, especially in the evening. In certain periods, usually every ten years, a ritual feast (slametan) is offered for renewing the roof covering of the house.

Taking care and maintaining the Javanese house in a proper and good condition is not simply for its use and function as the seat of its owner. Rather, the care is actually based on the respect and belief that the house contains benevolent spirits (danyang). A house without spirits is an empty being, which is not safe and not healthy. Making peace with spirits is actually the act of thankfulness to what have been given by the house to live in its spatiality.

Slametan is offered to demonstrate the thankfulness and wishing for the well-being of the family. Of course, believing in the spirits is only the way to respect the thing as well as being that for Javanese people everything has its own right and place in the universe (jagat). Living is always being with other beings based on mutual respect, regardless of its form and manifestation. Since for the Javanese everything is respectful being, setting up a home is not to dominate others. Instead, building a home is always to make up with others for bringing everything into a whole system in harmony. Every step and phase of building construction and its inhabitation is a request for safety and peace within the framework of ritual feast (slametan).

Dalem, Home as Inner Self

Harmonizing with place and society is nothing but bringing about mutuality and relationship from rude to fine, from raw to cooked, because home is built, sustained, and maintained and demonstrates the care for being and growing together in oneness. Home is experienced with rasa as striving for prolific gathering without self-interest as conceived in the Javanese saying, sepi ing pamrih rame ing gawe.

Silence (sepi) for the Javanese tradition is not forthrightly the empty realm. Rather, silence is the realm in which abundant creative power is. Oneness for Javanese culture must have been understood as the reality of their world. For this reason, the purpose of living is not to find the right or wrong path toward happiness. Rather, the purpose of living is sa’derma nglakoni, walking along the course of being on earth for growing with their understanding of wholeness. Hence, home for Javanese tradition is never a physically attached realm, but it is a harmonious extension of holistically embracing inner self (batin).

The very idea of home in the Javanese tradition is constituted by the sense of being able to listen to the voice of silence (swara ing sepi). Of course this is not a cliché credo for the sake of mysticism, as is often accused of Javanese thought. For the Javanese, silence is pre-conditional to come into the ability for absorbing and witnessing the phenomena of beings in wholeness, in which every being reveals in its own property and capacity ready to come into play in the worldly existing system. In incorporating silence, the Javanese house is characterized by tranquility of interior sphere with ambient light and earthy color of wood. Most materials used in the house are absorbent for color and light. The daily Javanese sphere of home is likely dim, gloomy, mysterious, and portentous, instead of vibrant, tumultuous, and cheerful.

How can home in Javanese culture come to terms with silence? Of course, the way to understand the state of being home as well as silence is never mundane. Instead, it is somewhat insightful and intuitive in the context of rasa. Accordingly, the idea of home is constituted by rasa that leads us into the state of being kerasan, meaning being intact with harmony. All this is believed to be attained with identifying one’s inner and outer self, then liberating oneself from any mundane attachment, including one’s self-consciousness: who and what am I. Being intact with harmony is not simply coming into agreement with others, but it is necessarily carried out for the purpose of living that grows beautifully.

According to Javanese belief, the silence would never have been experienced without being able to liberate ourselves from pamrih (interest and desire). In other words, detachment of mundane and bodily yearnings leads us to the open being without self-consciousness (rumangsa). Nonetheless, it is not to say that sepi ing pamrih means being without awareness (eling). This state of being open is known by the Javanese people as eling that might be properly understood as being free from any preconception and prejudgment. Only in association with eling, the Javanese home becomes be accessible, meaning being able to submit the self-consciousness to the awareness of wholeness of beings. In a practical way, the holistic awareness never comes into being without being able to see others within us. It is the reason why the Javanese physical home is called dalem (inner container).

The innermost of human being is batin (inner self), from which the phenomena of the world are projected in various forms, scales, and dimensions. Dalem is actually the projection of batin in its intimate realm of wholeness in which a family lives and grows. Architecturally, dalem is a conceptually generating system of home from which the institutions of Javanese settlement is physically developed. The construction of dalem itself is to put everything into wholeness within a spatial boundary. Architecturally, the sense of dalem is constituted by a visually established domain within a surrounding wall. The sense of being at home for the Javanese tradition is arrival inside the enclosure of dalem, which is actually the house yard.

The construction of the wall surrounding the house yard has been conveyed by Javanese tradition to articulate dalem in its urban context. The reason to establish the surrounding wall is not simply for the reason of security and safety. Far from its practical purpose is its symbolic form of town, khita or kuta meaning literally fortified domain, since house stands in harmony with its compound, and its town. Nonetheless, the idea of dalem with its surrounding wall (pager) is mostly to be built in an urban context. In rural areas, dalem is represented by the main domain of the house, which is constructed under the roof. Generally, the idea of dalem is the place where people find their respective seat in society. The importance of dalem lies in its capacity to establish people’s identity with their seat in the society.

Architecturally, building form and its construction seems to have set the stage of the rebirth of the world with its multiplying message of the necessary oneness. Then dalem, house, or palace seems to have not established the daily activity, but that the theater of oneness instead. It is possibly the reason why the Javanese buildings and towns do not show an excessive effort to represent the built environment in the sense of object in the space. Instead, the built environment stands at the service of events of gathering.

The house as dalem is actually a symbolic form of the seat in society. Then its design and layout of domains are directed to perform the Javanese world coming into being within its territory. The events in the house as a socially growing institution of dalem are mostly related to the rituals of lifecycle from birth, initiation, marriage, and death. Dalem becomes the altar of house rituals. The mound of decorated rice called tumpeng or gunungan is usually put in the main area of dalem. The stage itself takes place at the front part of dalem, which is for the most powerful house owners built separately and known as pringitan. This hall is actually the stage for shadow puppet plays (wayang) with their audience sitting in the reception hall called pendapa. Thus, dalem as a house system is perfectly devoted as a stage of house rituals and ceremonies by performing wayang as its primordial demonstration of the Javanese world. The perfect house in the Javanese tradition is its capacity to carry wayang performance for wedding ceremony.

The performance of shadow puppet plays in a house is traditionally not only for leisure and amusement, but also for delivering traditional values and Javanese cultural identity. The event of wayang performance is considered the occasion for being in spiritual alignment with Javanese tradition. The house of occasion for wayang performance is the platform of social gathering and spiritual unity under the charm of dhalang (puppet narrator).

The performance of wayang itself is a dramatic presentation of Javanese interpretation of Mahabharata epic. According to its tradition, the story will be divided into three acts: the world in crisis, rivalries and conflicts for restoring order, and the way to success and its resolution. The most interesting part of the performance lies in the second act when the punakawans (court servants) come into play with jokes and humor. Laughter and joy are shared among the audiences during gara-gara (the performance of punakawans).

Actually, the messages of punakawans are the voices of people in responding to the current situation concerning social and political affairs. Regardless of its political affiliation, dhalang is supposed to bring his audiences into emphatic togetherness. Again and again, the message for the exigency of solidarity and unity is delivered by dhalang in a rhetorical manner. In the Javanese world this emphatic phenomenon of togetherness is understood as luruh. The concept of luruh designates the necessity for being submissive in front of the wholeness. Bringing people into awareness of oneness seems to have been established with wayang performance from generation to generation. Then the house becomes the center of the Javanese world during the all-night wayang performance.

House as a work of art is conceived in the Javanese tradition as an exertion that makes a contribution to the sustenance of wholeness under the notion of work as karya. Since work as karya is somewhat useful in enriching our world as the totality of beings, artists in the Javanese tradition are more or less wholeness workers that bring the events of oneness into being as truth. Consequently, the higher the worth of the work incorporating the world in its oneness, the more sacred is its presence. Court heirlooms (pusaka dalem) are the finest works of art and the most sacred things for the Javanese state.

Architecturally, the very idea of dalem does not have its essential denotation without having heirlooms such as draggers (kris) at its central innermost room (sentong tengah). Being covered and imbued with mysticism, the finest works of art are well associated with amulets (aji-aji) and the highest knowledge (kesekten). Every Friday kliwon evening of the Javanese calendar, all heirlooms of the house are subject to cleaning with flowered water. Of course, the smoke of burning dupa (incense) and reciting mantra from the Al Qur’an’s verses belong to the ritual of the cleanup ceremony. The Almighty God, gods, ancestors, and all benevolent spirits are included within the wish list and thankfulness after reciting the mantras.

The relationship between house and the appreciation for the works of art is thought to be the heirlooms that keep and guard the family from bad luck. Collecting other fine art might have been considered a part of sustaining its capacity as heirlooms handed down from parents to their children. That the house contains heirlooms (pusaka) in the Javanese tradition is considered essential for reminding of their ancestors. A house (omah) without any heirlooms is considered a container (wadah) without content (isi). The sacred room (sentong) might have been established as the altar of the house.

Being obsessed with the exigency of oneness brings about a collectively established way of expression in the arts. Design based on free idea is not considered to be fit in the Javanese community because of its strong commitment to individual style and performance. However, there is no proper word for design in the Javanese language. The nearly translated word for design is ngrancang, originated from the word ancang, meaning to take a step. Ancang in building construction is the condition of being prepared for further action.

Evidently, the design profession has not had an appropriate place in the traditional community except in the kraton. Needless to say, in the past creative and innovative individuals would have worked under pseudonyms or under the king’s name. Independent artists today may not belong to the Javanese court community, but they could have been considered the sustenance of the Javanese tradition regarding their way of holistic thinking based on nglaras (harmonizing with others). Hence, creativity and innovation are welcomed in Javanese culture within the framework of cultivating everything that is to improve and to elaborate indigenous traits and characters.

Assertiveness and overconfidence are unlikely Javanese artistic building expressions. Although most of Javanese literate people are, by their tradition and language, well-trained thinkers, stillness, persistence, and modesty become the signs of their scholarship. It must have been seen that enormous innovation was not accepted in Javanese tradition without traditionally shared values for the exigency of wholeness. The aesthetic charge that works in the architecture of settlement is in the exigency and framework of oneness (manunggal) in which the principles of conformity (cocok), modesty (sa’madya), respect for others (tepa selira), and peaceful togetherness (guyub), and harmony (rukun) come into important play in the composition, expression, construction, and habitation.

Regarding its strong commitment in conformity, to have a seat in Javanese society is to be fit in its conventionality delivered by its tradition and its language. Being strange and odd in Javanese tradition is perceived as nyeleneh, meaning crazy and loss of common sense. The principles of conformity, mutual respect, and harmony seem to have established in Javanese works of art whether in gamelan, music (karawitan), literature (sastra), shadow puppet show (wayang), batik, blacksmith, or in architecture. Harmony, modesty, mutual respect, and conformity are predominant and predetermined condition in the Javanese works of composition. The Javanese make these conditions within a single formulation: runtut, meaning to be proper and step by step for striving for the best and the ultimate.

The Javanese people are trained with their language to follow step-by-step courteous action based on respect for others. According to runtut, there must be no stone unturned for paying homage and respect to any step and person intact on the way toward goal. Thus, following the principle of oneness for works of composition is to stick with a proper procedure (runtut) in which respect, cooperative, and collaboration are highly encouraged and promoted. Arrogance, impudence, and underestimation might have been necessarily avoided and discouraged. The beauty in this sense is not somewhat explosive, individual, and solitary. Rather, it is to some extent musical and orchestral.

The access to recognize the beautiful thing is made possible by rasa, the concept of articulating feelings and emotions with respect to harmonious totality. Architecturally, a beautiful building is constituted by its harmonious proportions and modest expression that lead toward elegance, insightfulness, maturity, and modesty. The Javanese artistic way is likely to cook everything raw toward its ripeness gradually but persistently. Rush, hurry, and scurry are not likely Javanese. Instead, thoughtful, meticulous, prudent, and circumspect are highly regarded in Javanese culture. In dealing with form, construction, and decoration, Javanese architecture gives its priority to reticence.

Moreover, artistic expression in Javanese tradition is elaborated from its own elements and resources with respect to their genuine traits and contributions to wholeness. An aesthetically well-considered building is understood in its wholeness as apik (perfect, orderly, proper, and fine). The concept of apik is applicable for a building with clean, proper, and neat performance. Beauty in the sense of apik is stipulated with a well-maintained condition. However, the beauty of nature is never comprehended with apik, but instead with asri that pertains to the state of growing well, generosity, fertility, sustainability, and harmony.

In Javanese culture, building expression does not necessarily become pretty and fancy. Rather, beauty for Javanese is conceived as the sense of well-being that is articulated in the notions of rahayu, apik, and laras. Here, beauty is not simply appearance but reality that is constituted by a harmonious relationship between human beings and their environment. Therefore, the presence of beauty is not only to provide people with a cheerful environment, but also to be fit in socially established circumstances in the Javanese tradition. Fitting in socially established neighborhoods is articulated by the Javanese with the word ngalaras. The original meaning of this concept is the act of going here and there without any predetermined destination. Nglaras is a kind of roving in the sense of thinking and walking as well. The importance of nglaras lies in its identical relationship with the Javanese concept of dwelling as walking through the time (nglakoni). Similarly, both concepts do not have any pre-set destination.

To integrate the building in its social context is considered by the Javanese with nglaras that is more significant than to set individually profile. In this respect, the significance of the built environment lies in its commitment for oneness socially. Since place is the address for proper being, its significance is not simply a location, but a domain where phenomena of gathering take place. Place in Javanese daily life is understood as panggonan, meaning proper site for proper things in relation to the others (people, time, and character). Panggonan is derived from the verb manggon, meaning to settle down or to put something appropriately in a location. As most of the prefix pa give us the indication of being part of place, for example, pawisman (dwelling house), padunungan (settlement), padusan (public bath), paninisan (place for having breeze), pangomben (place for taking drink), etc., panggonan is to address everything that has already found its proper seat.

Living with a socially established seat in the Javanese tradition (mapan) is distinguished from having a place to live (manggon) because of its very idea of respectful standing. Mapan is derived from the word papan, the place that is originally made of timber. The other meaning of papan is shield and shelter. Thus, papan in the Javanese idea of dwelling has a special contribution to provide people with a shelter that allows for taking action and position. The idea of mapan etymologically is to be ready for further actions. This is the sign that the dwelling is an attitude to be ready and never feel tired doing the same over and over again.

As mentioned earlier, mapan stems from the word papan, that is, to articulate the place for proper living. In association with settlement, mapan is to describe the situation and condition of dwelling at peaceful, sustainable, and respectful form. Papan and panggonan have a similar idea in their capacity to accommodate dwelling. As in its language, the Javanese category for a living place is not differentiated by its utilitarian purpose, but by social status and position. Nonetheless, all these denote the readiness to accommodate any possible change and adjustment for opening the place. The Javanese priyayi people deserve their papan in urban core, whereas the commoners (kawula) are supposed to have their panggonan in rural area. In daily talk, panggonan is also to address any place to store something appropriately. Owning a home for Javanese is not associated with manggon but mapan instead.

House, Familiarity, Hospitality, and Habitation

One important question concerning the necessity for building and dwelling is to deal with the sense of home. What we would build and dwell in is nothing but the cultural things that enable us to constitute and recollect our sense of home. The Javanese sense of home is composted of the concepts kerasan and pomah. The idea of kerasan is derived from rasa, meaning feeling, mood, and sensibility, whereas pomah stems from omah (house), meaning being settled and having a seat.

Since kerasan is an impression of being able to deal with the environment, its experience is stipulated by the mood and feeling of being intact with the wholeness. The sense of kerasan lies in the fact that everything is environmentally close at hand and friendly associated with. On the other hand, pomah is the state of having seat in society. The very idea of pomah is not only having already settled in the environment, but also describing the state of being familiar with and fitting in the culture. Moreover, pomah is also to mention the state of being in a secure relationship with housemates and neighbors.

As mentioned earlier, the rasa in Javanese culture plays a very important role in the aspect of culture and daily life. Rasa is associated with the appreciation of aesthetic experience and social life. Since kerasan means being familiar and convenience with environment, which is experienced with emotive and intelligible intact with place, its very idea lies in owning the precious moment without rush and recklessness. On the other hand, pomah is the sense of being at home that is constituted by the identity of men and their place. The identity speaks of correlation of characteristics of place that fit with one’s environment. It is necessarily stipulated by the sphere of ayem (at calmness) and tentrem (peaceful and complacent). The sphere of cozy ambience is characterized by indirect day lighting, shadowy nuance, earthy colors, and warmth, which are typical and characteristic of the Javanese feeling at home. All this brings about the built environment in the background for possible events and happenings.

Although omah implies at the very least a roof over the head, its role in Javanese lives is far more profound than that. Omah-omah is to establish a family, whereas pomah is to articulate the feeling at home. Furthermore, the notion of wis-omah is to express the state of being familiar with something. The application of the concept of omah can be found in the following: bale omah for household, omahan for being like a house, diomahke for being domesticated, tamed, and civilized, and somah for spouse or married partner. The Javanese language is replete with allusions to omah, which means a nurturing and secure place where the Javanese social life began.

Pomah is derived from the ancient Austronesian word Uma, Umah, Omah, Humah, Rumah, meaning the world. The world in this sense is not understood as a globe or the whole earth. The world in Javanese Omah is the household with their land and people/neighbors. Pomah is the state of already being involved with the world on a certain site based on the learning process. The relationship of the Javanese people with their homeland as a physical environment is not a sentimental matter. The Javanese are taught by their tradition to always be ready for any possibility of living in the spirit of thankfulness. This is probably an acceptance with inner strength and faith in open-endedness nrima, the notion is sometimes misread and misunderstood as the ability to accept bad luck in terms of surrender or giving up without fight.

Omah in a Javanese town is territorially defined by the penyengker (rectangular wall surrounding the house). The definition of the house itself is not that explicit utterance because its essence lies in the idea of Jero or the inside or the inner part. Jero is the word to articulate the state of being inside. The other word for house is dalem, which is usually to address the residence of people with higher social rank and status. Dalem is literally to articulate the self, the inside, and first person as well. What is interesting to note is the idea of dalem in its spatial quality that embodies the center of a smaller system.

The thing of the house is not the building in terms of the shelter (papan) or the house (omah), as mentioned earlier, but the state of being inside beyond the border. The transition between the inside and the outside is designated by the entrance door (kori). The meaning of kori is life or source of power. The substance of this word is koripan, meaning the life. The question is: What is the relationship of the door and the life? Door is a transition from and to outside or to inside. The transition is possibly not perceived as a border in a strict and clear category. We experience the idea of continuum in different states of being between moving and stopping. Thus, the category of inside and outside is not only understood as a physical transition, but also as a sign of the life in between. Inside and outside are complementary and signify and articulate the life and the being in the world as well.

The idea of inside (njaba) and outside (njero) in the town scale and context is traditionally demonstrated by the town gate, known in the past as the seketheng. The word speaks of the beginning and the end of journey. Sekheteng is not a physical structure for making a demarcation between nature and culture. It is actually the opening of kelir (wall or curtain behind the gate). The seketheng stands to the kelir in order to articulate the state of sublime into and from a different realm. The other word for the gate is gapunten. The word is derived from punten, meaning apology and permission. The gate is the place to announce and to ask for blessing, apology, and admission. The word delivers the message of wisdom to recognize the essence of being in between. The important thing of the message is to experience the between as a condition of being aware (eling) that life is not the question of right or wrong, inside and outside, but open-mindedness and being humble with dignity. The Javanese culture sum up open-mindedness and being humble with dignity in one word: njawani or being Javanese. It is probably the most important thing for being preserved and conserved in the Javanese context.

The notion of njaba-njero is not simply a physical demarcation. Conceptually, the category of in and outside is to bring clearly to mind the capacity of inner self for setting up the realm of home. Njero is always identified with the inner self in its tranquility, tentrem from which njaba is able to define. Based on this peaceful condition, the njero is conceptually contracted and expanded to work out its relationship with the public world. Although the definition of njero is architecturally established with main house in a yard surrounded by a wall, the house remains open for any visit. Njero is seemingly not equivalent to private domain. Rather, this inside is spatially to articulate the capacity of power contained by its owner. The larger njero is, the more powerful is the capacity and responsibility of its owner to make oneness come true. The largest njero is, of course, the palace with its surrounding wall (baluwerti) that is to define its rigorous self-identity as the center of wholeness, instead of for defense.

As mentioned earlier, the idea of place in Javanese culture does not make any separation between form and space. All is inherent in the name or concept of the thing itself. Space and form/construction are not perceived as a territorially defined boundary, but as the platform where the thing shows itself as the daily use or event. Flexibility of occupation is architecturally demonstrated in the Javanese house provided that there are no specific assignments given to its space except its core (sentong).

In any rate of expression and representation, it is difficult to define place without being able to find the center at a permanently constructed form. The center is always on the move. If we find something that is possibly a center, then we find nothing but the hollow. Alun-alun is a traditional Javanese town square, which is meaningless and nothing without any religious events. The relationship between alun-alun and the mosque and the palace (kraton) does not come into the light without religious events and happenings. We will find nothing in the house if we cannot observe the center. The core of the house, called dalem, consists of mainly three rooms: sentong kiwa, sentong tengen, and sentong tengah. At the center of the house is an empty room reserved for heirlooms. The most important part of the center is when the bride of the house sits together with the groom during the wedding ceremony reception. This center is only dedicated to this event. The number of brides in the house corresponds to how many times the room is at the service of house.

Being aware of what happens right now for the Javanese culture is more important than any effort for anticipating the future. Being aware, called eling, does not mean ignorance to the future. The future is not conceived in a progressive and linear life course. Instead, the life is figured out in the sense of cycle of life with eternal repetition. The puppet shadow play (wayang Kulit) teaches us to understand how the Javanese people perceive their world that every occasion has its own lakon (scenario and figures). Any lakon is always open for identification with certain characters and heroes. For the Javanese, there is no any reason to worry because any crisis has its own heroes and solutions by its own nature. There is no reason to take unnecessary action, but eling (being aware of one’s own capacity and role in the society) and waspada (being aware, vigilant, and careful and able to see the differences) are absolutely indispensable.

The future of human living for Javanese culture would rather the question of walking through rather than that of pursuing happiness. Consequently, building and settlement in the Javanese world depends on how to exercise its indigenous capacity to embrace, to contrive, and to integrate everything that is coming into its realm. It is not to accumulate power and glory without being able to domesticate contemporary surrounding things, techniques, and institutions within the already-existing indigenous framework.

Building and dwelling for the Javanese tradition seem to have been established as the way to deal with contemporary challenges. All these for Javanese people are never out of commission of domesticating (njawake), literally meaning making it familiar with the Javanese way—because the idea of home for the Javanese does not lie in the physical representation, but it lies deep in their way of nglakoni and Urip sa’derma mampir ngombe: life is a walk and take a rest for a while for taking a drink, nor more no less. In this sense we learn the wisdom of the Javanese thinking that holds on culturally materialized things. All this is seen by the Javanese in a different way. In other words, the things are only the shadows.

The way to find a home for the Javanese is not a physical matter but an inner business of feeling in the sense of kerasan. The question is quite critical for those who hold the idea of building and dwelling in Western historic monumentalism, maintaining the authenticity of objects in the space according to its origin. For the Javanese tradition, living is walking through in a way not to hold everything passing by. It is, however, not to say that there is no lesson from the past.

The Javanese would rather develop their attitude to be ready for the right now rather than glorifying the past. For Javanese people, dealing with the right now is already to embrace the past and the future with the pragmatic concept of nglakoni, that is, the way for experiencing, owning, and comprehending beings as a whole. There is no “us versus them” in any level of being, whether as thought or as form and event.

The idea of home in the Javanese tradition is considered inseparable from the search for kalenggahan, a respective seat as a socially established position on the earth. Sitting in Javanese tradition is always poetic in the sense of lenggah or lungguh. Its meaning is never out of touch with the reflexive idea of being among others. Here, sitting is not simply the sign of position in social life. Rather, sitting is to reflect the awareness of being part of whole society with a particular role and contribution. Its idiom pertains to one’s occupation and profession within the sense of hand (hasta). In association with hand, sitting becomes the sign of the capacity and competence to do something in society.

Sitting in Javanese culture is articulated with the word lenggah, literally meaning being at a permanent position to carry out a particular assignment in terms of ngasta. Hence, lenggah (sitting) is never out of commission of ngasta (occupation). Since sitting is actually a sociopolitically dwelling position, the relationship between seat and occupation does not only rely on job and responsibility. Rather, in the Javanese tradition, this relationship is more symbolic in the context of authority depicted from society as a cosmic mountain realm. Accordingly, the seat in society (kaprajan) has its own level and capacity for making action and decision concerning wholeness. State officials are highly regarded persons. Consequently, their houses seem to have demonstrated their rank and status. Traditional houses for high-rank state officials are provided with a front hall called pendapa.

The hall (pendapa) is originally from the Sankrit loanword mandapa, meaning waiting hall in front of the temple. The establishment of pendapa is to demonstrate the rank and status of its owner in society. The simple hall is erected with a rectangular ground plan. The form and construction of the hall are called joglo, standing on four pillars called sokoguru, with its encircling sixteen columns. A simple pendapa has only a single crown (brunjung) and single robe (penaggap). The higher the rank and status of the owner in state bureaucracy, the more he can have its robes for his pendapa. The most important person in the Javanese tradition is the king (ratu). His halls (bangsal) are not only perfect joglo with two or three robes for its roof, but also vary in accommodating various rituals such as bangsal witana (reception hall), bangsal kencana (hall for court ceremony), bangsal srimanganti (hall for ritual visit to the king), bangsal manis (hall for court dining), and bangsal trajumas (hall for court wedding and court family rituals). Every building and hall in the Javanese house must have something to do with the necessity for rituals and ceremonies.

The use of platforms for table and seat place is a longstanding tradition for public gathering, even in the court. Nevertheless, sitting as dwelling is a respective aspect of culture, in its daily actuality there is no indication that the use of furniture is a longstanding Javanese tradition. We do not find the authentic Javanese word for chair except the throne (singasana), which is only for the ruler or a powerful landlord. In some respect the place for sitting in Javanese tradition is on patarana.

The patarana is, however, not the chair but a bank or platform (amben). As shown in the panels of Borobudur`s Karmawibangga, pre-colonial Javanese society did not sit on individual chairs but on the bank or on a platform. The art and arrangement of sitting are characterized with squatting (sila, literally meaning principle and fundamental). The concept of sila itself is originally Sankrit, but it has been elaborated in its use and context in the Javanese tradition as the respective position of sitting for assembly (rembugan) and social gathering for ritual feast (njagong slametan).

It is not by accident that the Javanese home does not have any specific dining area with table and chairs. There is no part of the house plan dedicated to the activity of dining architecturally. Sitting on the floor is indicated by the use of carpet known pranada (Javanese Kawi word). Although there is no any indication that the use of furniture has been established in the home in ancient Java, the use of a mattress known as prastarana (Sanskrit) is evident.

Architecturally, the pre-Islamic Javanese house might not have developed the use of furniture in its interior. Sitting on the ground or on the platform must have been the common case for pre-colonial Java. Beside the absence of furniture, pre-colonial Java must not have had established domains in its house realm with specific daily designations, such as bedroom, washroom, living room, dining room, etc.

Even though the Javanese house seems to not have been designed primordially for daily living, there is the almost forgotten place for preparing the food for a ritual feast known as prantunan, originated from the word rantun meaning to stop by or to come over. Since the place does not have any architectural structure, but is determined by event, today its domain has fallen into oblivion.

The idea of prantunan, however, means the state of being impermanent. Actually, prantunan is dedicated to providing the house with a place for preparing a feast. If we look at the Javanese house of today, preparing food takes place in pawon, which is located at the rear part or side part of the house. The service area in the Javanese house is located in a relatively private and restricted zone. The Javanese tradition gives the name to the supporting part of the rituals with the word wingking, meaning rear or back. Kitchen, stall, storage, garage, laundry, and workshop belong to the rear category of the Javanese house. Visually, such buildings must have been arranged behind the house.

Although privacy in the Javanese house does not reveal bluntly in association with room of activity, its phenomena manifest during mealtime. The inside and outside category comes into play when family members gather to have their breakfast, lunch, and supper. In rural areas, family members sit together by squatting on the platform in the main room of the house. Meanwhile, in urban areas, the Javanese family father has his first turn for having the meal. Traditionally, the mother does not eat together with his father, but stands by to serve him. She and her children eat together after the father leaves the dining table.

In the modern Javanese family, egalitarian dining has been fully accepted. However, they always keep respect to the father or older members of the family. They let the older persons in the house have their first turn for the meal. Of course, the fixed positions of sitting at a dining table must have been established as a part of family ritual. Father always has his position at the head of the table. Close to the father of the house is the lady of the house, who sits at the left side of her husband. In their traditional house, the family dining area is located in dalem. There is, however, no fixed room for dining. Importantly, the area where dining takes place will be prepared for other activities after the mealtime is done.

Sleeping for the traditional Javanese family takes place on the platform (amben) located in the inner part of the house close to the hearth (pawon). Parents and children do not sleep on the same platform. They sleep, however, in the same area. If the house has girls, they would have their sleeping platform in the core of the house (sentong). Meanwhile, boys sleep on the platform in the front part of the house. Although the modern Javanese family now lives mostly in Western style houses, there are still traces of sentong, which is traditionally dedicated as a spare respective room. They may call such a room a guest room or mushola.

Since the purpose of the Javanese house is originally designed for rituals and ceremonies, its space is mostly a hall without fixed walls. The elaboration of layout for accommodating rooms comes into being when they encounter modernity. The modern Javanese house keeps the character in the use of traditional perfect form of three divisions of pendapa-pringitan-dalem. The adjustment of activities into the three main parts of the house has been made to be fit with contemporary and urban lifestyle.

The hall in most cases remains open without a wall. The use of daily life is to receive the guest for a temporary visit. The second and the third part are usually integrated for a living room, a dining room, and sleeping rooms. Kitchen and pantry are commonly set up in the back area close to the servant quarter. The modern Javanese house usually has an adopted pool and recreation room. The sense of home in the broadest context is not restricted only by dwelling place. Since for Javanese dwelling is living with others, every place has its own spirits and is not restricted to the visible, but it also comprises the invisible. There is no place that does not contain a spirit. According to Javanese belief, places are containers (wadah) and the spirits are their content (isi). The spirits (danyang) are believed to guard the place so that every place has its character and purpose in the universe. There is no empty place on earth because spirits are like air and invisible incorporating natural forces. This belief dates back to pre-Hindu-Buddhist Java that pays respect to the forces of being. The important places for Javanese are identified with the concept of angker, meaning holy, sacred, mysterious, and sanctified. Sacred places have their own guarding spirits that do not allow people to harm their territory.

Clearing and building natural place are due to deliver ritual offering (sajen) and ritual feast (slametan), designating reverence and respect to their existence. Offering and feast are considerable negotiation and permission for making up the environment for human living. Offering with a sacrifice of the head of water buffalo is given for the establishment of settlement, bridge, and dam. At any rate the offering and feast are dedicated to bring about the relationship between human beings and other beings in harmony.

Since place is meaningful because of it is publicly well known, collective memory is necessarily established. The collective memory of place in the Javanese culture has to do with the events and happenings in which spiritual leaders are involved. The Javanese way to institute collective memory is by regular collective visits on the great days of Javanese calendar. Since memory in Javanese is recognized in the concept of eling, meaning reminiscence, awareness, concern, and mindfulness, memorial place is to remind them the respect to their spiritual leaders (panutan) for their blessing and benevolence. Graves (sarean, meaning place for rest and sleep) of spiritual leaders are conceived as places with full blessing. Since death in the Javanese tradition is never conceived as the termination of being, maintaining and conserving memorial places are actually never out of commission for sustaining a personal relationship with the dead.

The place has no special location in the Javanese collective memory if its events and happenings have nothing to do with their spiritual leader (panutan). The idea of panutan is to remind people of the leadership and prophecy that have brought them into oneness. The word panutan is derived from anut, the word for to follow or following. Anutan means the teaching, stream, discipline, and beliefs. Memorial place is understood in the Javanese tradition as place with trace (patilasan).

Patilasan is originated from the word tilas, which means clue, trace, hint, remain, and rest. What does memorial place (patilasan) have to do with trace? Does the Javanese culture perceive the memorial places as the traces that allow people to remind their leaders and teachings? It is probably the reason why the memorial place is meaningful when the religious events take place on the site.

The visit for rituals or respect to the site is understood as sowan or ziarah, a spiritual trip. What they preserve and conserve is an event that reminds them to the spiritual leader concerned. The site of the occasion is perceived as tilas, which is believed and ought to be recovered as a whole meaningful world by the rituals. The other places that have nothing to do with the traces to their spiritual leaders will not be considered patilasan but as tengeran, meaning landmark or sign for orientation.

The place where various landmarks are is the town (kitha or kuta). The mountain and sea are natural landmarks that are traditionally integrated in the layout of the town for giving orientation. Gates (plengkung) and town wall (baluwerti) are important landmarks besides mosques and alun-alun. Although in post-colonial Javanese towns there are some monuments, traditionally they do not build monumental buildings that do not have to do with their living tradition. The only monument that stands in town is an obelisk-like stature called tugu. In the town of Yogyakarta, this building is used as the reference point while the king’s meditation takes place in the front hall of his palace, gazing through alun-alun along the court boulevard.

The Javanese idea of town is derived and developed from the Indian and Southeast Asia mainland experience: kuta, meaning the site inside surrounded by the rectangular wall and canal. Besides its sociopolitical meaning as the seat of their ruler, the Javanese people understand town as the site where the peken (permanent five-day market) has been established. The idea of town as the physical interpretation of kuta is not adopted in a strict sense. Then urbanism in Java since the eighth century is difficult to be reconstructed. Until this time, the ruins of Prambanan and Borobudur remain silent to give us the clues on the structure of old Javanese towns. We are still waiting for the finding on the origin and structure of the Javanese towns since the beginning of the eighth century.

What we experience from the Javanese on the fact that the town is that where peken (traditional market), alun-alun (town square), and kraton or kabupaten (regency) have been instituted and sustained. The most important aspect of town life is the peken. Its origin comes from the verb mapeken, meaning assembly or meeting and gathering. In contrast to village (dhukuh), kitha (town) is established with a relatively permanent market: peken. The other word of peken is pasar, possibly derived from the Persian bazaar.

For the Javanese tradition, it might be that the same things happen in other countries, peken (market) is not only the place of transaction, but also the site of social interaction and amenities. Leisure, gambling, amusement, entertainment, and popular urbanity in the Javanese tradition are developed from this institution. On the other hand, court becomes the mother of cultural institution for conserving and preserving tradition in documented form and in a sustainable system.

Compares with court as the center of high culture, the market is more egalitarian, which is apparently recognized from the use of its applicable language. The development of urban culture in the market is mostly based on freedom of expression and exchange. Regardless of its association with spontaneity, vulgarity, and crudity, the market for a Javanese town is an actual center of sociocultural and economic life. Although the existence of the court is never out of Javanese people’s concern, the reality of the Javanese public world does not exist at its court, but instead in their peken.

Chapter Five

Javanese Pride, Patronage, and Prophecy

Political Culture in Indonesia

Although the reformasi has achieved some changes in Indonesian political institutions, the development of social justice is barely realized today. Why is that? This chapter argues that whoever in power does not change Indonesia toward a civil society based on democracy and social justice because their political culture of despotic paternalism has not yet changed fundamentally. The problem of civil society in the largest archipelago lies in the transformation of its cultural values from hierarchical feudalism to democracy. Hitherto, the status quo in the archipelago still holds fast to the ancient values based on patronage, pride, and prophecy that defy the democratic concept of power and equality. The predominant Javanese tradition—from which Indonesian political culture has been shaped and established—has always been a challenging factor for the process of democratization in various levels and occasions. This chapter is an attempt to explore the aspects and elements of political culture that can decelerate and hold back the agenda of reformation for a civil and democratic society based upon sovereignty of law.

Javanese Culture in the Indonesian Context

Originally, one important agenda of the political reformation (reformasi) was to carry out the transformation of Indonesia as a whole; it comprises the power relations in the political, bureaucratic, judicial, and social systems. The fact is that reformasi has not yet radically changed the political culture of the state for nepotism, corruption, and collusion. Consequently, poverty, disparity, and abuse of power are still obvious on a daily basis and mostly without legal consequences. Establishing social order based on rules of law has been mostly challenged by patrimonial establishment in the executive, legislative, and judicial systems.

The question is why the process of democratization is hardly able to change Indonesia toward a civil society. What are the actual forces that can undermine the democratization of Indonesia? To what extent is Javanese political culture dominant in the reality of Indonesia as state and nation? The questions lead this chapter to look closer into the nature and roots of political culture that have historically shaped and established the Indonesian state and society. Moreover, this chapter is an attempt to unmask the aspects of power that work, shape, establish, and sustain the political culture in Indonesian society.

Besides political and historical events, local concepts and their context became the primary source of reference for this study. Local concepts have been indispensably important for theoretical and textual interpretation with respect to their contextually originality. Hans Georg Gadamer (1976/2008) points out the importance of the relationship between language and the life world that provides us with the awareness of history. Accordingly, man as well as society is a linguistic being. Then the importance of local knowledge lies in the capacity of local language as the resourceful system for its society that enables them to grow and to develop their culture and tradition. This fact is not new for anthropological and philosophical studies. Clifford Geertz (1983) has pioneered studies on Indonesia based on local concepts and their relations to sociocultural phenomena. Further explorations on the significance of local knowledge have been presented by number of scholars (Bicker et al. 2004) that show its potential resources to deal with global crises.

The focus of this chapter is to divulge and to dismantle the local concepts, which are associated with phenomena and ideas of power relations. This chapter will emphasize its analysis on the reveal of those concepts in relation to the political affairs. In doing so, politics and language are conceptually inseparable from their culture and society. Studies on politics of post-Suharto Indonesia have been presented by numerous scholars (Emmerson 1999; Budiman et al. 1999; Bertrand 2004; Bertrand 2013; O’Rourke 2002; Eklof 2002; Metzner 2000; Bunte and Ulfen 2008; Nyman 2006). Their works are not only important for understanding the development of democracy in the country, but also give us a new horizon for further investigations. One of them is to find out the relationship between political developments and the existing political culture, which is predominantly influenced by the Javanese tradition. After having been oppressed by the regime of Suharto for more than three decades, the reformation in 1998 was undoubtedly the beginning of a new era of democratic Indonesia, though it was undoubtedly challenging and uncertain. The reformation was expected to redefine and to reevaluate the elements and structures of political culture toward an open and democratic society.

Indonesian society is historically and socially divided; it consists of the ruling class (priyayis, penguasa), and the people (rakyat, hamba). This antagonistic system works subconsciously in Indonesian societies, from village to the capital city, as well as from ancient times until today. Since the dualistic system has worked until today, the access to state politics is limited to only the elites (Anderson 2007, pp. 1–69); the culture does not tolerate any opposition. Grassroots politics has been traditionally perceived as threat and disease against the state and its authority.

Even though the Dutch scholars have detected the dualistic organizing principle as an ancient social structure that works in most Indonesian ethnic groups (Adam and Anwar 2005, p. 336; Jo-Sears 1993, pp. 123–25), contemporary studies on Indonesian politics pay less attention to considering this pervasive division. In such a sociopolitically divided group, the change of political system does not automatically transform its political culture unless the societal structure as a whole changes; it is unlikely to happen in post-Suharto Indonesia, especially under the administration of Megawati, Susilo Bambang Yudhoyono, and Joko Widodo. Nyman in her 2006 study points out that after the 1998 reformasi there was no official plan made for Indonesia’s future democratic transformations. Joko Widodo does not say something concrete on the direction and transformation toward democratic society in his presidency agenda of Nawacita—Nine Priorities (Kurniawati 2015); his agenda is to establish a good governance.

Political Culture as Theoretical Considerations

The relationship between culture and politics for Indonesian studies is neither a new approach nor a field of research. Benedict Anderson (1972/2007) was the prominent scholar who introduced the importance of Javanese political culture in Indonesian politics (Suryadinata 2002; Anderson 2006). Accordingly, the idea of power in Javanese culture is the source and reference of social order and ideals that works in Indonesian politics until today. The dominance of Javanese culture in Indonesian politics is self-evident on a daily basis for power relations; Javanese courtesy and manners are practiced as unspoken language in the bureaucracy and in the educational institutions.

In this chapter, political culture is understood as a system of concepts, symbols, values, customs, manners, behaviors, and frameworks on public decisions, policies, and actions (Fagen 1969). So far, the regimes in Indonesia have utilized and legitimized political culture over their nation and society in order to establish, maintain, and sustain their status quo (Pye and Verba 1972). As a cultural system, political culture is historically developed, elaborated, enhanced, adjusted, and modified in favor and in support of the current circumstances of regime and society. Political culture is probably an effective approach in analyzing and in understanding politics and the behaviors of power (Bryder 1989; Solomon 1971, p. 8).

Since the values of politics are indispensable from local culture and tradition, in order to understand the contemporary development of Indonesia, it is argued that such transformations, toward a civil society based on social justice, are only possible if they involve the whole members of society. As a matter of fact, the current democratic process only takes place for public elections, but it does not go any further and deeper into the practice of governmentality for an open society.

Theoretically, political culture is a relatively permanent framework. It upholds and sustains the principles, ideals, and essential values of state and nation. This implies that the radical change of political culture is unlikely to happen, which is considered an uprooting process of the current concept and actuality of the state and nation as a whole. Although in the era of reformasi some new institutions and procedures for democratic governance have been installed and implemented, the historical roots of political culture are still preeminent in daily political practice because they are well preserved and conserved by bahasa Indonesia and Javanese manners as the basic structure of power relations.

Nevertheless, political culture has something essential in incorporating the ideals of collective consciousness that underlies the existential structure of the institutions and relations of a state and nation. Accordingly, changes and transformations of political culture are not only determined by the persons in the status quo, but also by the elites of Indonesian society. They are mostly still comfortable with the old practice and values of fatherly patronage, pride, and prophecy; all these become the structures of patrimonial political culture in Indonesia.

Undeniably, patrimonial power is more than just authority and hegemony. Rather, it comprises all ability to create and do everything, which is exercised as the will and effort to move the world according to patrimonial preferences, such as top-down operative, collectivist, conformist, and loyal. Since the meanings of the world are preserved with the local language, the reformation of political culture necessitates the transformation of its language of power in terms of spoken language and gestures. Indonesian spoken language in this case is, in some ways, interpersonally egalitarian, grammatically tolerant for errors, biases, and tenselessness; this language is more supportive for democratic communication. However, communicative gestures of manner and behavior speak louder than spoken language concerning the patrimonial values system. To a certain extent, Javanese concepts and expressions are used to underscore the patrimonial tones in the context of appropriateness and acceptability. Even though the Javanese language is not the official language, the Javanese feudalistic and hierarchical manners and comportments fit well with the state codes of bureaucratic governance. For centuries, the Javanese political culture has disseminated into the state bureaucracy of modern Indonesia since its early conception in 1945. Furthermore, in the predominant Javanese populations, the Indonesian language as lingua franca has already been molded and adjusted with feudalistic, hierarchical, bureaucratic, and patronage culture. As a matter of fact, the locality of language is not merely a communicative medium of political culture. Rather, the source of values and meanings distinguish a cultural geography from another place.

Since culture is a resourceful system of values and ideals, all citizens collectively share the values and ideals of political culture; it is an integrated part of state ideology and national interest. Therefore, the necessity for political culture is obviously useful for the establishment of regime and nation as a whole. Since political culture is an outcome of the historical development and evolution of a nation, its function is not only philosophically referential, but also pragmatically directive in dealing with the business of power and state politics. Even though in many ways power and culture might be interchangeable in establishing the ruling mainstream, the ideological coalition of both is probably the most effective force and system for any political program.

Political culture is perhaps only workable because of its collectively shared vision and notion. In order to have such a vision, the regime will take a lead either by oppressive method or through a democratic process. The problem of shaping and establishing a new political culture lies in its elites and mainstream, whether they are willingly and at the capacity to retrospect, to redefine, to reorganize, or to reevaluate their system and business or not. Since democratic political culture is ideologically not compatible with paternalism, its practice is not likely desirable. In addition, the mainstream of political elites is still comfortable with the traditional patronage of priyayis over the rakyat. In many ways, reformation in this country is challenging for its ideals and programs in shaping a civil society based on democracy and social justice.

The Javanese Political Culture

There are some impediments that are able to decelerate and discourage the process of democratization in Indonesian politics. All this is likely related to the concept of power, by which most decision making for the whole nation and state is done. First, the concept of power in the archipelago is deeply rooted in the paternalistic patronage (perwalian kebapakan) of traditional communities and societies. Politically, patrimonial culture has been embodied in the prehistoric village polity of wanua (van Naerssen and de Iongh 1977, p. 37). Paternalistic patronage is not only a matter of values through education and socialization since early childhood, but also an issue of societal way of life based on the dichotomy of leader (panutan) and follower (pengikut). Since paternalistic tradition has been part of Indonesian political culture, strong leadership is important and mandatory by which building a shared vision and a collective mobilization is made possible. The tool for such a vision is a political credo or rhetorical slogan (semboyan), which incorporates a thematic spirit for movement called semangat. The semangat is the content of any political movement and leadership. Without this, political leaders or organizations do not have a substantial motivation.

Hitherto, the mainstream Javanese population and Indonesian society has held and sustained a monolithic system of paternalistic tradition, which is essentially feudal and patron-client oriented. The attitude toward paternalism is demonstrated with a loyalty to the status quo as long as it gives them the sense of peace physically and spiritually (pengayoman). The sense of pengayoman lies in the ruling power’s capacity to give a security and stability for their daily activities. The root of the Javanese word pengayoman is ayom or ayem, literally meaning being protected for peaceful mind and unitary harmony. The paternalistic leader is supposed to be a protector and provider of harmony and unity for the people. Failing to give such conditions costs the power of their pride (wibawa) as a leader.

Second, the intentionality of kuasa is the grandeur of prestige (pamor, gengsi) and pride (wibawa, harga diri). All this has nothing to do with dedication or devotion to public service. On the contrary, the idea of kuasa is more about the privilege for mobilizing resources for the majesty of the ruler (raja) and his/her state (negara) and nation (kerajaan). Even though today the title of ruler has been changed from raja to presiden as well as from kerajaan to negara, the meaning of their concepts remains mostly the same with only a few modifications here and there. In the Indonesian context, kwasa is similar to the Indonesian word kuasa, literally meaning right, capacity, ability, authority, and prestige. Accordingly, the notion of kuasa is neither power in terms of wealth nor that of knowledge. Rather, the concept of kuasa emphasizes and underscores omnipotent authority and the prerogative capacity to say right or wrong as well as proper and improper.

Wasa in ancient times or kwasa in modern Javanese language is a concept of power for controlling, ruling, and dominating others. Its practice highlights and emphasizes the notion of authority, which is absolute and non-negotiable for their decisions and actions. As a concept, kwasa is concrete as the privilege of who sits on the throne as ruler. Whoever is able to get that seat is the person with the eligibility as the holder or keeper of kwasa. Traditionally, to be a ruler with such predicate does not require legitimate process and procedure; one just needs the courage and capability to get it from the status quo by coup d’état or by smooth transition and succession. Even though in post-Suharto Indonesia a democratic process has been taking place for the transition of power, it does not mean that illegitimate transition of power could not happen.

Third, as person in power, he/she is the atasan, superior authority or trustee, that has the right to exercise the language of prophecy (turur, pinutur). Atasan is not only superior for giving instructions or directions, but also traditionally supposed to be the fatherly figure that is trustworthy for any issue and matter, from personal to official matters. Loyalty and devotion to the atasan or penguasa are mandatory, that is, not about morality, but humiliation, disrespect, and disgrace of power. Under the notion of kuasa as well as kekuasaaan, the authority is the divine master (gusti, tuan, datuk) and all people under his/her supervisor are actually slaves (kawula, hamba, abdi). The person in power (penguasa), either as a higher-rank state bureaucrat or as a police officer, has the capacity to be a dominator. His/her main job is not to serve his/her constituents, but to rule his/her supporters and followers.

In daily business of patrimonial power, the prophetic language belongs to the opening speech of official occasions. Any commencement of public initiative and action is due to get the restu (approval, blessing) as well as the petunjuk (advice, direction) and pengarahan (direction) from the local ruler (penguasa local). All this reflects and represents the practice of political culture of theatrical stage based on pride, patronage, and prophecy that penguasa, the person in power, is the pompous director of such public occasions and events, while others are his/her humble audiences. He/she is also the one who has the privilege to open the events with a ritual ceremony by hitting the gong or by giving an opening speech with prophetic messages. In many cases, the rulers play as a social therapist on sociocultural issues; in some cases, he/she is also the priest for the judgments of moral issues.

Making public events or occasions is not simply a social gathering. In Indonesian political culture, such an occurrence is perceived as the birth of the cosmic realm that needs a ritual blessing, permission, and acknowledgment from local authority (penguasa setempat). Such a customary request and recognition is not merely for politeness and courtesy. Rather, it is a necessity for being in alignment with the local world; the local authority is conceived as the one who holds and controls the order and harmony in his/her territory. In terms of kuasa and its realm (kekuasaan), all public happenings and events—from social gatherings and fundraisers to academic conferences—must be under his/her restu, meaning blessing, control, mercy, knowledge, and approval.

In terms of kwasa or kuasa, to stay in power means to be able to mobilize people for grandeur self-cult (praja binatara). Performing state rituals and spectacles is one of the most important displays of power or might as kwasa. This includes mass mobilization and crowd control. The expansionist consequence of the concept of power as kwasa is not simply for political influence and economic benefit. Rather, this need is deeply rooted in the ruler’s desire for grandeur spectacle (grebeg) of their power. This includes mobilizing people for doing something collectively for the interest of state and nation. Recently, some political leaders and CEOs have appealed to their followers and subordinates to wear batik clothes on certain days in during the week. As president, Yudhoyono (2004–2014) mobilized Indonesian citizens everywhere to wear batik clothes every Friday starting on October 2, 2009 (Detik.com 2009). The master of mass mobilization in Indonesian history was charismatic Sukarno; he used to mobilize people at every national event and official occasion with his rhetorical oration. Being a great orator, Sukarno could spellbind his audience and possess them by his immense charismatic personality (Cederroth 2004, p. 82).

In order to imbue their power for mass mobilization, a spiritually unified credo (semangat) is created and publicly announced as the spirit of movement. The origin of semangat is actually a personal power (prabhawa), which is inherent as inner strength, strong character, and courage. Prabhawa manifests in charismatic personality and charm that enable him/her to attain kwasa. Even though prabhawa is often associated with individual talent and personal gift, the Javanese tradition teaches the elites to attain such power by various methods and trainings such as celibacy, meditation, and fasting. All this is done to mystify his personality and keeps him/hers away from the real world, with less talk and highly developed self-control. In order to keep his/her pride (wibawa) and image (prabhawa), a raja or ratu as ruler is in the role of pamong praja, that is, able to be an integrator, protector, and savior of state and nation as a unitarily cosmological system.

Over the course of time, the daily world is possibly contaminated by spiritual pollution (sukerta) and needs cosmic cure (ruwatan) for its safe and healthy form and state of being. On a smaller scale, such a cure is made at the household and community level, which is known as slametan. Ruwatan and slametan share their common ground on the symbolic boundary maintenance for the safe and united world (Geertz 1960, p. 14; Mulder 2005, p. 89). The presence of ancient tradition for the rebirth of the world is signified with the performance of puppet shadow plays (wayang kulit). The puppet master (dhalang) not only plays his/her role as a conductor and director of the puppet show, but he/she takes charge as the ancient priest in the performance of the special myth of murwakala, a story of the birth and growth of the monstrous god Kala (time), who poses the power to eat everything in the world. Ruwatan and slametan are nothing but the Javanese way for the acknowledgment and respect of the power of time. Unity and harmony are not simply understood in terms of interpersonal relations among people, but they are also perceived as the validation, affirmation, and confirmation of the commitment and loyalty of all people (praja/kawula) to their ruler (raja/gusti). Criticisms and oppositions against the government in various ways are conceived as humiliation, disloyalty, and disrespect to the person in power as well as to state and nation. Such conducts are considered polluting and destabilizing to the cosmic order.

The concepts of power and danger have been introduced by Mary Douglas (Douglas 2002, pp. 117–40) in her studies on cultural values, knowledge, and cosmology. Douglas traces the different concepts of clean and dirty in cultural context that is deeply rooted in the symbolic boundary of safe cosmological realm. In Javanese context, purity is deeply rooted in the concept of the pure form of the world bhawana. Accordingly, the pure form is safe (aman), manageable (tentram), well-ordered (tata), healthy (rahayu), and prosperous (gemah-ripah). Ironically, the power to maintain this purity is known as wasa binatara, literally meaning celestially terrifying and frightening power.

Since the notion of power in Javanese as well as in Indonesian culture is concrete (Anderson 2007, p. 22), the seat of the ruler becomes an important showcase of hegemony. Power is concrete because it is represented by the seat or throne of the ruler. Then taking over the seat is claiming the place as the ruler. Still, concerning its concrete form, power needs to show up in terms of spectacular happenings or grand exhibitions. Therefore, the intentionality of hegemony in Javanese tradition is not about the exercise of power concerning daily business of politics but concentrating and preserving power that is (Anderson 2007, p. 5). The intentionality of power is to perform this power with a grand spectacle for the ruler cult. It is not primarily to entertain the public but for self-grandeur of his/her majesty. In doing so, the ruler moves upward from mundane reality to transcendental realm. He/she makes a case as the untouchable being; he/she is able to watch over his/her people from above.

In the Javanese context, the concentration and preservation of power are culturally related to the idea of unpolluted wholeness and the unity of transcendence and immanence (sejatining utuh lan manunggaling gusti kawula). Impurity is considered associated with fragmentation (modal-madul) and disarray (gonjang-ganjing). Impurity is not only polluted with destructive components, streams, and movements against the unity of Javanese world, but it is also somewhat cosmologically degenerated toward chaos and the state of getting lost.

In Indonesia, power and culture has always been an interchangeable pair of the hegemonic concept of kuasa. Accordingly, the establishment of power as kuasa, kekuasaan is not merely the institutionalization of authority, but it also comprises a spiritual and cultural hegemony with the strong sense of purpose for unitary nation and state. This sense has been historically shaping and establishing Indonesian political culture. In the daily practice, the state negara is bureaucratically despotic that exercises and pertains to the power kuasa in terms of agung-binatara, meaning “the terrifying authority” (Mudjanto 1986). Accordingly, the rulers are neither public servants nor representative leaders, but simply the dominator or master in terms of penguasa; the state officers are penguasa, which literally means hegemonic person. In the broadest sense, the notion of penguasa is actually a dominant agency that is bigger than our life. Such an agency is capable of taking over your actions and decisions because you are his/her slave (kawula or hamba). Ordinary people are perceived as hamba yang didaulat, meaning slaves under the mercy of their master. Penguasa is the blessing giver as well as the mercy provider.

The other notion for penguasa is pemerintah, from the word perintah, literally meaning command, instruction, order, and direction. A state officer is conceptually penguasa and pemerintah. The reformation movement has not yet successfully transformed the notion of government from its hegemonic position to public representative job. Anyone who is in power is expected to be the godfather of their kingdom. Accordingly, a leader is necessarily dominating (menguasai) in their intentions and actions because they are perceived as someone above average in their knowledge and sight on the circumstances and situations of their realm (dunia, wilayah).

Power as kuasa or kekuasaan is due to cover up their flaws and weaknesses with everything they have. They will defy any attempt to disintegrate and disgrace its existence and status quo in terms of purity of power. Stepping down from power because of scandal is neither a graceful exit nor an elegant solution for the hegemonic concept of kuasa, but shameful and disgraceful instead. Instead of resigning, kuasa urges persons in power to save their image (citra) and pride (wibawa) as penguasa or omnipresent authority. Blaming their subordinate officers for being responsible is the most common practice for such scandalous and outrageous abuse of power.

Since the Javanese political culture is sophisticated in its concept, form, and practice, its infiltration into Indonesian political culture is delicate and refined by means of courtesy and graciousness. The hegemonic model of Javanese despotism and paternalism is never to exercise direct confrontation. Putting others under the Javanese control is identifying, synchronizing, and integrating the strengths and weaknesses of others within the Javanese system. This is actually the practice of syncretism in the Javanese tradition in terms of laras, nglaraske, literally meaning harmony or to harmonize. In this sense, the notion of democracy will be harmonized with the Javanese concept of saresehan and rembugan or the Indonesian notion of musyawarah (open deliberation) toward mufakat (agreement) (Tjiptoherianto 1996; Rais and Najib 1999). Accordingly, the original idea of democracy has lost its essential and original concept of individual freedom; the Javanese and Indonesian version of democracy is a mere vehicle and way to the power for the unity and harmony of the nation and state. In many ways, musyawarah is another word for a deliberation toward alignment with the political will of status quo or the mainstream. Subsequently, whoever is in power is eventually due to perform the unity and harmony, that is, not to carry out democracy in respect to individual freedom.

Since the Javanese culture is by theory and practice highly sophisticated, the demonstration of the model is never obvious but subtly symbolic and allusive. The despotic paternalism will be cultivated with various tactics and strategies. In Javanese tradition, such ways are well known as disanake (being treated as their own children and family) and dijawake (being educated or tamed as Javanese). Bluntly speaking, it is to say that the person has been under control according to Javanese norms and manners with or without force. Accordingly, the person in power will embrace every potential opposition with an appeal to the intentionality of state and nation as unitary system. He/she will do everything in his/her power to eliminate and to diminish any possible resistance and conflict from the very beginning.

Power and Impurity

In the Indonesian context, purity (bersih, resik) and danger (bahaya, baya) are neither simply categories of moral affairs nor hygienic values of public health. Rather, in Indonesian political culture they are crucial issues in identifying individuals or groups in the enemy of the regime in power. The slogan says bersih diri bersih lingkungan, literally meaning purity of the self and environment from any pollutant against the wholeness of the world. In the broadest sense of the words, the credo implies the political requirement for being clean from any indication of resistance, disagreement, and opposition against the status quo.

Even though the notion of purity (bersih) is to address the governance with public accountability, its conceptual origin remains morally significant in the cosmological sense of the harmonious world. This phenomenon is obvious in daily politics that the governance for the harmony and unity of nation and state is more important than that based on law and order. In other words, a clean government is more intended to establish a unified nation and state free from any opposition, rather than to set up the rules of law with many opponents. Moreover, in terms of the Indonesian political culture of kuasa, clean authority is seen as a strong and solid status quo, which is able to stabilize the unitary system of state and nation. The sense of clean here is more about freedom from any disease and malady of disintegration and disgrace, rather than that of corruption, collusion, and nepotism.

In Javanese political culture, purity and danger are culturally related to each other by one concept, namely power in terms of kwasa/kuasa and prabhawa. Accordingly, the problem of purity and peril is neither about justice nor about the legitimacy of power, but simply about their relation to the accretion and stability of hegemony. This implies that the ruler has more to be concerned about how to save the world from impurity and danger. In Javanese tradition, crises are traditionally needed as opportunities for new leaders to emerge; as the Javanese saying goes, jer basuki mawa beya, meaning there is no worthiness without cost, danger, and crisis (Jakarta Centre for Strategic and International Studies 1991).

The case for taking over power with force and violence has been a common practice since ancient times from the Sanjaya and Shailendra dynasties to the modern Indonesia state. Ken Arok murdered Tunggul Ametung in order to get the power from him in the thirteenth century. Such a taking over of power brought Tumapel into crisis. A similar case happened in 1965 when Suharto took over the power from Sukarno, with a mysterious letter of transition of power known as supersemar (Roosa 2006). In the midst of turmoil, no one questioned the legitimation of how he came to power. For the Javanese people, such turmoil or upheaval is perceived as a sign of impurity in their world. People’s intention was to wait for a savior of the crises. Suharto played delicately to rise into the occasion. Such an occasion seemed likely to be repeated in 1998 when Suharto was away from the country.

Inner struggle for power among elites in the armed forces attempted to create the scenes of national crisis with social and political upheavals, such as the Trisakti incident and anti-Chinese riots. As a matter of fact, the internal power struggle among political and military elites at the time had not successfully come to end with a winner. From the Javanese political culture’s point of view, the reason is clear because no one at the time was able to play their role as a knight in shining armor (satria piningit). In many ways, the 1998 power struggle was not the case for Ken Arok, Jaka Tingkir, and Suharto in Javanese history. Even though the 1998 crisis of power was an opportunity for taking over the national leadership, the elites were not able to utilize the event because they did not have the capacity to do that. From the Javanese point of view, Habibie is not a strong ruler, and Wiranto is not a tough leader either. On the other hand, Prabowo Subianto is neither a convincing knight nor an inspiring organizer. Even though Wiranto was in the position to take over Suharto’s seat, he did not have the guts and solid confidence to do so. Moreover, Wiranto is Suharto’s protégé, to whom he should be always grateful and respectful (Emmerson 1999, pp. 309–10).

Indeed, the political culture of Indonesia is a dynamic process that has been shaped and established by the status quo. During the last decade of Suharto’s reign, such a political culture was challenged by the need for change. Since Suharto as person was unable to replenish and purify his world, his light had gone dim and he was not able to mobilize his resources for economic recovery due to Asia Financial crises in 1998. He resigned as the ruler, which is not because of a scandalous accident but simply because he realized that he no longer had the pride and prestige needed in terms of power as light. He had to give away his seat voluntarily to someone else, otherwise somebody would take over from him violently and cruelly.

Political culture plays significant role in determining criteria for what purity and danger are. In Javanese tradition, the one who is able to bring down the status quo is the one who is capable of restoring the world order, which is mythologically well known among the Javanese people as satria piningit (Soesetro and Al Arief 1999; Pamungkas 2008). One important role of such satria is not playing hubris. On the contrary, he/she has the guts beyond average people to fulfill the call of duty and claim his/her power without mercy to his/her enemy. Cruelty, bloodshed, and violence are part of this claim. Again, as the Javanese saying goes, jer basuki mawa beya (every successful achievement requires terrible sacrifices). Bearing this in mind, it would be fallacious to assume that purity and peril are morally religious issues. On the contrary, power in Indonesian political culture has always been the source of the world order and wealth. It is metaphysically concentrated and conceived as the center of spectacle.

Exercising power in daily Indonesian culture is not about ethical right or wrong (Geertz 1960, pp. 77–85; Hadiz and Dakhidae 2005, p. 239) but about the pride and prestige of the ruler over the ruled. Accordingly, the ruler is perceived with a perfect image of protector, savior, director, and benefactor. Saving and establishing the image from weakness and flaw is very important at any cost. Nonetheless, personal distance between the ruler and the ruled is needed; the paternalistic patronage is likely a fit relationship. Beyond its political formality, such dichotomy does exist on a daily basis; the ruling class (priyayi) remains powerful over the ordinary citizens (kawula, wong cilik) at any level of community activities.

The root of political culture of the societal dichotomy between the ruler and the ruled is historically shared, shaped, and cultivated among the Malay, Bugis, and Javanese people. The unity of the dualistic system is ideologically incorporated by the ruler with a semangat in the sense of a collectively purposive spirit. Both are considered essential in understanding how the ruler crafts their “imagined nation” and its establishment in real politics. Semangat is the essence of political agenda in order to fulfill the intentionality of power as the light of his/her kingdom. Accordingly, semangat is somewhat unifying, encouraging, illuminating, inspiring, motivating, and mobilizing.

Negara is nothing but the container and vehicle of semangat with two levels of practicality. First, it is at the real politics that negara is the state body of polity for national affairs in undertaking domestic economy, foreign relations, sociocultural matters, national security, and defense. Second, it is at the ideological level that negara is the means for transforming semangat into the spectacle of power. In order to achieve the grandeur spectacle of negara, semangat, as ideological content, is necessarily pure and cosmologically unitary. Purity and unity are necessary conditions for the mobilization of all resources, free from doubts and resistance.

The ancient political culture in the archipelago was rooted in Austronesian wanua, a concept of village republic as the world, which was traditionally established by paternalistic leadership with the support of dualistic and antagonistic groups: the landlords and elders. Semangat fuels and imbues the village world with a unitary concept of cosmological aesthetics. Accordingly, unity and harmony are identical with the beautiful and prosperous world. Across the archipelago, the spirit of unity (utuh) and conformity (mufakat) are evident among ethnic groups of Aceh, Malay, Java, Minangkabau, and Bugis. Errington calls these societies—with former Indic states—centrist archipelagoes because they are preoccupied with unity and view fracture and divisiveness as either the result or cause or both of personal illness, community misfortune, or political failure (Pamberton 1994, p. 243).

In the Indonesian context, semangat is deeply rooted in the ancient belief of the unitary power of state (negara) and nation (bangsa). Unsurprisingly, the modern state of negara incorporates semangat as state ideology of pancasila. Semangat is perceived as spiritually motivating and emotionally moving people toward unity and integrity. Based on the beliefs in semangat, there are three principles that stand out as the virtues of Indonesian traditional political culture.

First, harmonious unity is conceived as the basic principle of political culture in the archipelago. The intentionality of the political world is to bring people together as a reality of house and home. In discussing the political ideas, issues, and affairs, political elites have to keep in mind that unity (kesatuan) and harmony (selaras) in the community are not put at stake (taruhan) and at risk (bahaya). Unity and harmony are conceived to be indivisible. They are traditionally maintained as the sense of community (hakekat bermasyarakat). Any move to disintegration and discordance is considered against the system as well as the world. In many ways, any new idea and influence will be tested within their consequence, whether for solid and strong community or disarray and disintegration.

Second, strong and firm leadership (pimpinan kuat dan tegas) is always in demand. It does not matter whether it is right or wrong, but simply that it is about real politics that work for the interest of the mainstream or the elites. Strong and firm leadership is conceived as the spearhead of the community in moving forward for their pride (kapriyaian/priyayi, harga diri) and prestige (kaprajan, gengsi) as nation and homeland. A leader is respectful because he/she is not only restrained, brave, and a risk taker, but also never hesitant in making decisions. It does not matter that he/she is stubborn (keras kepala) or cruel (keji) to their enemies. A leader is someone who is able to lead people to make them proud of themselves as nation and respected as a country; they do not really care about how such a leader comes to power in terms of a legitimate way or not.

In many ways, the demand for strong leadership could lead people to a fascist nation with blindly patriotic fanaticism as demonstrated by Sukarno (1945–1967) and Suharto (1967–1998). Such early indication of nationalistic populism has been noticeable in recent Indonesia under the reformation era, as shown by the formation of populist and anti-liberal movements from Megawati’s Democratic Party of Indonesia in the Struggle and Gerakan Indonesia Raya Party, Gerindra, in 2007 under the leadership of Prabowo Subianto.

Third, searching for common ground is the aim and the light at the end of the tunnel of any political deliberation, otherwise strong and authoritative leadership will take charge to determine the goal. Searching for common ground is usually conducted with meetings and deliberations (musyawarah). Consensus, called mufakat, is actually neither achieved with deliberations nor with negotiations that are principally to set aside any interest against the political mainstream. Traditionally, musyawarah is a paternalistic persuasion and education of elders to those who are still not on the political mainstream. And mufakat is actually acceptance to be on board with the mainstream. Again, it is not about proper or improper, but simply the necessity for keeping the image and pride of unity and respect to the status quo.

In many ways, consensus is the Holy Grail of Indonesian politics in order to achieve the homogeneity of power. Since power (kuasa, kekuasaan) is conceived as politically controlling and dominating authority, unity of mind is the necessary outcome. There are many ways to achieve a consensus. The most favorable is creating a common enemy in terms of ideology or political stream. In doing so, the power has public justification to do everything they ought to do for their status quo, with purity and danger as their categorical norms.

Based on the three characteristics mentioned earlier, Indonesian political tradition is culturally apt to paternalistic patronage, which is based on privilege of nobility and seniority of age. The patronage is in many ways pyramidal with hierarchical structure. Young leaders are not able to go further only on the basis of achievements. They have to convince their elders of their capacity to lead during the crises. Otherwise, they have to wait for a good chance to come.

Impurity and Cosmological Totality

Purity and danger are concepts of power to justify the three values of the political culture mentioned above. Politically, purity (bersih) means neither correct nor proper, but simply right on the track in line with the currently working ideology and hegemony. Despite the diversity of faiths, ethnicities, and origins, the world as a political reality is conceived as a homogenous system of hegemony. The source of this homogenous hegemony is unknown but believed to be spiritual energy called semangat or kesekten in the Javanese tradition.

Originally, purity as the state of being safe has been a traditional condition for a village. In order to ensure this state, villages in Java and most parts of Indonesia have to offer an involuntarily collective work for village care for hygienic and spiritual purpose. The work called bersih desa is actually not only cleaning, replacing, and repairing buildings and sanitations, but also spiritually replenishing and rejuvenating the village as the world. Bersih desa has a double purpose, which is for maintaining agricultural reproduction and for keeping hospitable relations with the local spirit world. The village communal meal (slametan or kenduri desa) is part of the care for village well-being and celebration. Indeed, bersih desa is the act of purification for the rebirth of the village as the world. All villagers are due to participate in the occasion. Only sick persons and children are free from active involvement. As a matter of fact, the act for the purification of the world is to maintain, to establish, and to sustain the unity of the ruler and the ruled as a whole.

So far, the tradition of bersih desa has been in effect until today. The best time of bersih desa takes place in every village, towns and city in the archipelago as an integrated part of preparation for the annual commemoration of Indonesia’s Independence on August 17. The day is the special event of the nation and state that potentially unifies and gathers all citizens as a whole. Villagers are encouraged and mobilized by local leaders for community service as well as for renovation for their homes and places. Various activities are performed with its peak on Independence Day.

Historically, the festivity of Independence Day is deeply rooted in Singasari and Majapahit tradition. According to Negarakertagama, the festivals and celebrations were culminated during the reign of Hayam Wuruk that took place during the month of Caitra (March–April). The state held a grand meeting and reception of all tributes from its subordinate kingdoms, regions, and territories (Leur 1955, pp. 7–10). In the ancient Javanese period prior to ninth century, one such annual celebration was known as the sima ceremony. It is the time when lower kingdoms, regions, and territories of the state were due to pay homage, tributes, and taxes to the king. Their presence in the state capital was the sign of loyalty and obedience to the ruler.

At any state occasion, the king or queen is actually the only spectator of his/her own show for his/her splendour, power, and gratification. Beyond him/her are the supporters and performers of the event. The director of the state theater is his highest rank of state priest, maharesi or mahapandita. The purpose of all these performances is likely nothing but the self-gratification of power, in the name of cosmic purification and the rebirth of the world. As a matter of fact, state occasions are the events that show who is “in” and “out” of the circle of power. For the ruling elites, the event is their show that the state and nation is in orderly structure (mapan), prosperous vitality (kerta raharja), and spiritually united (manunggal).

Impediments of Reformasi

As mentioned earlier, the concept of “clean” or “dirty” is not about moral value, but simply politically favorable or disapproved. During the New Order era, liberalism, communism, and socialism fell into the category of dirty, whereas centrist nationalism and state ideology’s loyalists were conceived by the regime as clean. Today and in the age of reformation, the mainstream and Indonesian elites still maintain the concept of “clean” and “dirty” based on the bottom line concept that “clean” or “dirty” has something to do with the mainstream ruling class. In this sense, danger (bahaya) is not simply unsafe and threatening for the nation or state, but to what extent its influence endangers the pride, patronage, and prophecy of the ruling mainstream.

In dealing with danger, the mainstream ruling group will do everything it can in the name of the unity of state and nation. In Javanese language and tradition the practice for defeating an opponent is known as ahangkara, meaning absolutely dominating and putting under control. Traditionally, the Javanese ruler is entitled to have ahangkara because he/she is the one who represents the divine on earth for peace and order. In terms of kwasa or kuasa, the Javanese power will use all possible means to put everything under control on behalf of peace and order. One well-known tactic in the Javanese tradition is apus karma, or subtle scheme or approach. The personal life of the enemy will be studied to know his/her strengths and weaknesses. The enemy’s weaknesses includes maen (gambling), madon (love affairs), mabuk (drinking), maling (stealing and cheating), and madat (taking opium or drugs). The famous Javanese story on apus karma was practiced by Senapati. As the first king of Mataram (1584–1601) in Kotagede, he had to defeat his opponent Ki Ageng Mangir Wanabaya. Senapati sent his own daughter, Princess Pembayun, as a dancer to seduce Mangir and force him to pay homage to the king after their marriage. The king invited Mangir and Pembayun to visit his palace. In the kraton, Mangir was forced to submit his famous spear and to kneel down in front of his father-in-law. Senapati killed his son-in-law and buried his dead body in two parts, one in and on outside the kraton’s wall. In doing so, Senapati was able to eliminate the danger to his status quo. The message of this story is clear that the status quo does not care too much about family matters, violence, and cruelty; those who are against the status quo are due to face death because it is perceived as a disease for the unity and integrity of a state as a big and conformist family.

For people in the archipelago, post-Suharto Indonesia is the era of reformation. Even though it is not clear in which direction the reformation was going, political leaders in the country—from nationalist, pragmatist outlook to Islamic fundamentalist followers—did agree on the notion of reformasi as a new beginning for post-Suharto Indonesia; it was the way out from the hegemonically despotic and paternalistic system. However, this was not the case. Under the presidency of Abdurachman Wahid (1999), the unity of state and nation of Indonesia was cosmologically considered as in crisis. The cause of this unrests and disarray was not from the opponent of the nation-state, but the chieftain of the state: Wahid as the president of Indonesia. In the Javanese perception, Wahid was supposedly not acting as a Javanese ruler who provides people with the sense of stability and harmony with careful judgments and subtle gestures. Instead, Gus Dur or Abdurrachman Wahid often made controversial statements and judgments that jeopardized the conformity and harmony of the Indonesian people.

For the Javanese people, Wahid or Gus Dur is an anomaly and a temperamental figure that was not fit to be father figure of society. He was neither a father figure person (bapak, pamong praja) nor a subtly authoritative man (penguasa). In truth, regarding his mismanagement and administrative incompetence, Abdurachman Wahid was an unlikely president from the Javanese perspective of political culture. Despite his reactive and controversial comments on social and political issues, Gus Dur was a well-known Muslim leader with a moderate outlook. From the perspective of Javanese political culture, the removal of his presidency by the People’s Representative Assembly in 1999 was reasonable because he did not fit in the picture of the Javanese ruler. This was also the case for Habibie and Megawati. Concerning political culture, they were considered too weak to be Indonesian rulers, especially in dealing with oppositions and resistances (Forschee 2006, p. 22).

The most important agenda of reformation is to establish a political and economic stability. Stability in the Indonesian language is conceived as mantap, literally meaning permanent, firm, consistent, convincing, stable, and strong. Such stability is crucially conceived as the actual measures of the daily experiences of ordinary people on the street. The government and its institutions may change, but if political stability is not achieved, state and nation are in peril. As a matter of fact, economic improvement and justice depend on this stability. In Javanese and Indonesian tradition, this political stability is superficially understood as stabilitas nasional. The deep structure of the concept of stability lies in the notion of kuasa or kekuasaan. Unitary state and nation are always the primary concern of status quo in terms of kekuasaan. Berkuasa implies commanding, dominating, and controlling the state, nation, and its resources so that they are not in danger. That is to say that the nation and state are not in impurity.

Indeed, the actual politics of reformasi is to make a radical transformation for the previous political culture. Even though the urgency for the sovereignty of law and democratization is on the agenda of reformation, both are not seen as a priority of the ordinary people. The outcome of the four public elections of 1999, 2004, 2009, and 2014 has shown us that the expectations of political stability are still high prior to dealing with economic recovery, unemployment, and poverty. For this stability, a strong leadership is needed. The New Order under Suharto (1967–1998) had successfully integrated the Javanese political culture into the Indonesian political system that order, stability, and growth are essential conditions for national integrity and identity. Political disarray or disorder, such as in the end of Suharto’s reign and the crisis of Wahid’s term, have been seen as a process of salvation that the world would be back in order and harmony again. These messianic beliefs are not only shared by the Javanese population, but also by most people in the Indonesian archipelago (Kroef 1959). The impeachment of Wahid and the unsuccessful attempt of Megawati at reelection until the election of Joko Widodo in 2014 confirm that the political culture of messianic savior of national stability is still on. Accordingly, people go to ballot in the hopes of satria piningit, a distinguished knight, or Ratu Adil, the king of justice who acts as the savior of state and nation from chaos and trouble (Geels 1997, p. 75; Choy 1999, p. 127; Sindhunata 1999, pp. 20, 84, 85).

Political stability has been a lesson learned from Indonesian culture and tradition on the concept of home and dwelling as sitting. Being stable (mapan) is stipulated by the state of having a seat or occupation (kedukukan); it is not surprising to address the citizens with the word penduduk because sitting or duduk is not only a body posture of staying. It is culturally the sign of having a home with a socially respectable standing. Political stability for Indonesian culture is the existential state of being because it gives people a secure environment for their well-being.

Moreover, for the Javanese ruler, having a seat (kedudukan) is neither a public service nor a social responsibility, but simply a powerful position as well as occupation for establishing stability and order in his/her kingdom. Sukarno and Suharto are probably the best examples of modern Indonesia’s leaders who recognize the sense of seat in relation to power. Suharto maintains the record for his almost thirty-two years in power because he knew that power is equivalent with the authority to maintain the order and stability of his kingdom; if it is necessary at any cost and with any means. Unlike his two predecessors mentioned earlier, Sukarno, Suharto, Wahid, Megawati, Yudhoyono, and Joko Widodo did know the sense of kedudukan, therefore they acted not only as secular leaders, but also as commanders in chief for spiritual matters. Javanese leadership should claim their position as traditional rulers who value the stability, unity, and order of state and nation in terms of cosmological realm and reality.

Whoever has the Indonesian presidency, he/she emphasizes the notion of continuity as a sign of stability. Even though he/she is the only leader who envisions a better Indonesia, he/she does believe in the end that the world is in perfect harmony; any crisis and disorder is only the way to remind people that there is time for redemption and salvation. Javanese leading figures barely adopt and accept an egalitarian and democratic way of life on a daily basis: a patrimonially ordered system should be in place. Only in such a system, the pyramidal structure of the harmonious world is cosmologically in order and in harmony. The Javanese leaders from Sukarno to Joko Widodo comfortably used the language of prophecy in their state speeches and official addresses to their audiences with the words saudara or saudari that sound like the connotation of subordination. In addition, they were never comfortable being addressed by their first name without the attribute of “Sir” or pak or bapak.

In Indonesia, one is never a person without a power of seat (kedudukan). Based on the political culture of patronage, pride, and prophecy, he/she deserves the prefix of bapak or ibu prior to their first name. In the language of pride, patronage, and prophecy, ordinary and common people are like the ocean of the unknown supporters, though they will be addressed as brothers (saudara-saudara) or sisters (saudari-saudari). From a language perspective, a democratic society in Indonesia is a challenging goal of reformation.

Unsurprisingly, Yudhoyono (2004–2014) and Joko Widodo (2014–) contain their party’s mission and vision with semangat reformasi, the spirit of reformation, that is actually in the sense of leading back to the original formation of unitary state and nation as declared in the 1945 Constitution (Partai Demokrat, Indonesia n.d.). Accordingly, reformasi is understood neither as a change nor a transformation, but renewal—in terms of readjustment and revalidation—and sustenance of Indonesian struggle from the colonial times until today. The very idea of nation-state in the 1945 Constitution is integralist. Supomo—one of those who drafted the Constitution based on his expertise in customary laws of Indonesian ethnic groups—believes in the necessity for unity and harmony based on family principle (azas kekeluargaan) (Bourchier 2015, p. 220), which is integralist and totalitarian through deliberations toward consensus. The relationship between the government and the governed is like father and son, that is, essentially paternalistic (Silalahi 2003, pp. 139–40). In other words, the nation-state of Indonesia according to the 1945 Constitution is conceived as a big family of various ethnic groups that value the unity and harmony of the whole people more than individual human rights. In addition, democracy in the Constitution is with condition concerning the wisdom of people’s representatives. It is worth noting that the word wisdom here gives room for local manners and values.

Even though his agenda of reformation for clean government and sovereignty of law remains less improved than Habibi’s, Wahid’s, and Megawati’s terms of presidency, Susilo Bambang Yudhoyono has been trying his best in those aspects. The real pressure of the improvement is actually the free press and the open public opinions since the fall of New Order. Nevertheless, on human rights and justice, from the administration of Habibie to Yudhoyono and Joko Widodo there is no sign of action and commitment toward justice based on universal human rights. Violence against human rights—from the 1965 massacres, East Timor, Papua, Aceh, the Trisakti killings, to Munir’s murder—remains in darkness and far from the light of justice. The question concerning justice and human rights will be handled by whoever is in power within the framework of family principle that upholds and keeps the unity and integrity of the Indonesian nation-state and society out of crisis and disarray.

In dealing with the unity and integrity of the Indonesian nation and state, the reformasi movement is likely not to change the fundamental principle of traditional concept of Indonesian political community; this option is considered a risky choice because it puts the sense of unity and integrity of the Indonesian nation-state and society in jeopardy. There is less room for a process of redefining its nation and state based on universal human rights. Reformation does not intend to transform the patrimonial society of Indonesia as a whole. It is not only risky, but also culturally unnecessary. As long as people—as represented by parliament members—are already seated comfortably on their seat, the politics of unitary state and nation goes on as usual.

Indeed, for human rights and social justice, the transitory process from an authoritarian to a democratic system is needed in which new institutions, regulations, and laws are not merely established and enacted, but are also tested and validated. So far, such a transformation is actually not able to radically change the minds of people from a unitary concept of state and nation to a federalist model. Despite the decentralization of governance, the post-Suharto national leaders still hold the concept of a strong nation-state based on the patrimonial system of central government that bureaucratically controls and supervises political and economic policies of development in the provinces, regencies, districts, and villages. The military and judicial structure is still implemented as a reliable organization. The sense of unity and integrity has been and still is the most important thing of nation-state as a big and harmonious family. Deep inside his heart is the Javanese perception of the world buana or bhawana as a well-ordered system of beings, which is only sustainable because it is conformist, clean, and free from any kind of opposition and resistance.

Traditionally, the Javanese ruler is the caretaker of buana as a unitarily harmonious realm; the head of the nation-state is not only a secular leader, but also the spiritual conductor of state ceremonies and rituals. Taking care is keeping it in a manageable control for his pride, prestige, and legacy. His first priority is to ensure that the state and nation are in a good unitary shape. In order to achieve this consolidation of power, he will do everything in his power to gain broad political support from his former rivals. The move toward a unitary system of state and nation is subtly enforced by a spiritual credo of semangat in various forms such as political consensus or state ideology of pancasila.

On development and modernity, the post-Suharto Javanese leaders hold the notion of progress, which is focused on economic growth and prosperity based on national integrity and harmony. The subtext of his cabinet composition shows the concept of unitary state and nation with a centralized power at his hand. Accordingly, power is the light of development that elucidates and illuminates its realm with a radiant intensity from center to periphery. Papua and other Eastern Indonesian provinces have been mentioned by post-Suharto leaders as regions with special needs for development acceleration. The regions, which fall into category of the left-behind provinces that are actually not just because of their less environmentally developed condition, but also due to being less culturally connected with the Javanese values system and tradition concerning the family state in the context of statecraft style and political manners.

Although there were formally more than thirty-four political parties participating in the 2009 public election, only nine parties were qualified to have their seats in the House of Representatives. What is missing here from the current president’s agenda is a unitary ideology. In the traditional sense, Javanese national leaders like Sukarno, Suharto, Wahid, Megawati, Yudhoyono, and Joko Widodo share something in common on the necessity for a strong ideological leadership in unifying all political streams and groups under a solid creed such as Pancasila for Sukarno and Suharto. Pancasila is not only an ideological platform that unifies people in the Indonesian archipelago, but also the transcendental structure of conformity for the family state concept of Indonesia.

Based on their ideology, the nine prominent parties of post-Suharto Indonesia can be grouped into two main streams: the nationalist groups (Demokrat, PDIP, Golkar, Gerindra and Hanura, the Nationalist-Islamist, and PAN), and the Islamist parties (PKB, PPP, and PKS). As in the Suharto New Order era, the political streams in Indonesian politics remain the same with its core in secular nationalist streams. Of course, Demokrat, PAN, and PKB play their roles at the centrist faction of the mainstream. They stand in the middle between the Gerindra-PDIP with nationalist supporters and PKS-PPP with the moderate Islamist devotees.

Besides the mainstream of political culture, there are, indeed, sub-political cultures that are advocated by various groups such as intellectual, military, news and media, and non-government organizations. Each of these is able to express and articulate their concern and thought freely. However, in order to make ideas work in the system, they need to work together with the representatives of parties in the House of Representatives and the government. As a matter of fact, the most effective agent of reformation is the print and electronic media from which the freedom of speech and expression finds its way to watch over the business of politics. The biggest threats for journalists in the era of reformasi are no more laws and regulations, which are intentionally designed by the executive and parliament (Romano 2003, p. 169). Rather, in the perspective of Indonesian political culture, press is conceived as an agent of liberalization or communism.

Hitherto, the notion of liberalism and communism in Indonesia is conceived like a disease that might lead the unitary system of state and nation to danger. Open criticisms and free opinions on politics are based on liberal ideology, which is unfit with the hierarchical custom and principle of family values (azas kekeluargaan). Such critical attacks will be perceived as disrespect and disloyalty to the father of the state and nation. On the other hand, communism is associated with atheism and classless society, which are not in the favor of the mainstream of political culture. With liberalism and communism, the mainstream of political culture is in danger because both are ideologically destructive for paternalistic and despotic hegemony.

Unlike the aptly fascistic regime of the New Order, the leaders of political parties in the reformasi era are collectively and individually committed to the freedom and democracy in their movement. However, their primary concern is still to establish the stability in various areas. Strong government is still in their view. This implies that they will unite to stand for or in favor of a centrist ideology. They do not tolerate any rightist or leftist group growing and getting established. The presidency of Wahid was the best example for this tendency; it was against the likely leftist Megawati candidacy. Even though Megawati was eventually to replace Wahid in 2001, her party did not have the choice to work together with the centrist parties, Golkar, PAN, and PKB, for the unity and harmony of Indonesia as nation and state.

Nevertheless, Yudhoyono, Megawati, and Joko Widodo-Jusuf Kalla share something in common on the unitary state and nation prior to anything else. There is, however, no single word in their political agenda addressing universal human rights as their main political concern. The absence of human rights—as the main agenda of politics of current administration—is unsurprisingly apparent because such an issue will bring about the unity and stability of current political patronage, pride, and prophecy in jeopardy.

The disengagement and discontentment on the outcome of reformation are inevitably increasing in significant quantity, which is shown from the numbers of the undecided voters; golput in the 2009 public election was higher than 2014 (Detik.com 2014). The Joko Widodo factor was probably the case; the enthusiasm for going to public ballots was high because Joko Widodo and Jusuf Kalla were public figures with populist programs and without direct relations to the New Order regime. Indeed, some significant reforms have been made from 1998 to the present, especially for liberating the press and the media to express their minds and facts lively. However, most political parties in the country are still comfortable with the ancient political culture. The only true supporters of reformasi are the press and the media. The role and the function of the press and media would be the determinant agent of reformasi in advocating and disseminating human rights and democracy as pillars of civil society. Nevertheless, the most important part of reformation is likely the transformation of relationship between state and capital (Robinson 2009). Socioculturally, such a relationship has not yet been exercised on the basis of the democratic mechanism for public accountability. Until recently, the cases of corruption show how personal relationships between capitalists and individual political bureaucrats remain less changed. The root of this lies in the Javanese concept of the world that is constituted by the unity of duality: manunggaling Gusti lan kawula; the concept affirms the reality of a wholeness between the ruler (ndara or bendara), and the ruled (kawula). The unity between the patrons and the clients has been incorporated in various ways, forms, levels, fashions, and styles of Javanese people. Other ethnic groups in the archipelago share this cosmological concept of dualistic unity in terms of father-children relationships (hubungan bapak-anak buah). In other words, patron-client relations have been socioculturally working in various aspects of Indonesian society from ancient times until today. This is one of predicaments of process toward democratic society in Indonesia.

In rural Java areas, the patron-client ties have been practiced as part of agricultural production. In Javanese villages, the patron-client relationship between landowners and landworkers builds a social bond within the framework of a big family. Such bonding constitutes a political and economic cooperation by which the practice of slavery becomes out of the question. This does not, however, say that there is the absence of class, but builds a form in which classes work out their relationships and seek political and economic accommodation (Robinson 2009, p. 216). Vertical mobility for class struggle in Javanese villages does not find its smooth way in rural Java (Wertheim 1969). This is because the patron-client relationship between priyayi and kawula has been developed as a synergistically binding system for political and economic interdependency. In so doing, the boundary of classes becomes unclear (Mortimer 1969). The key point of this traditional bond is the human relation of familial solidarity in terms of sanak-semanak. In the Indonesian context, this principle is known as azas kekeluargaan. The Javanese people understand the dichotomy of patron-client (priyayi-kawula) as a whole of a cosmologically, politically, and economically functioning relationship. This relationship is demonstrated by Javanese people with the practice of gotong royong and sambat soyo (Henley 2007) when their community deals with crises, dangers, natural disasters, and other communal works. The collective mobility of the Javanese people is not about class struggle, but more about care for their unity, integrity, and harmony as a community.

In the urban context, the patron-client relationship comes into play in politics and businesses. The patrons control the access of resources while the clients are in charge as laborers, mediators, advisers, guards, and supporters of production and services. During the Dutch colonial times, traders and commercial activities fell predominantly into the hands of local Chinese merchants (Wertheim 1956). The patron-client ties between politicians and businesspeople have been continuing from Suharto’s New Order era to the era of reformation according to Transparency International’s 2014 Corruption Perception Index, which puts Indonesia at 107 out of 175 countries (Transparency International 2015); the 629 cases in the same year, which have been handled and processed by Komisi Pemberantasan Korupsi, Corruption Eradication Commission, involve three ministers, forty-five high-ranking officers, and one high-profile member of parliament (Hukum Online 2009). Movement toward democratic society in Indonesia crucially needs to work out its framework from patron-clients relationships to civil democratic relations based on the rules of law. What has been achieved by the post-Suharto reformation is a democratic process of governance through transparent and fair public election. The transformation of society, in the business of government from the national to the local level, has not yet been working effectively from patron-client relationships based on personal preferences to democratic connections according to laws. In other words, post-Suharto Indonesia needs to change the cultural perception of populations from patron-client duality to citizen. This is what Joko Widodo understands in his 2014–2019 political agenda as the mental revolution because it is about the transformation of mentality of his people from priyayi and kawula to equal citizen (Kompas.com 2014). He does not speak loudly but his way, style, fashion, and manner of leadership demonstrates what a citizen is.

Concluding Remarks

The problem of actual reformasi lies essentially in the language of bahasa Indonesia, which is, in many ways, accommodative for the house of pride, patronage, and prophecy. The old saying says bahasa menujukkan bangsa: local language designates its nation. Seemingly, any movement against the mainstream of paternalistic political culture will be confronted with counterattacks with traumatic stigma of instable democracy like in 1950–1955 and anti-populist communism like in 1965. Even though the reality of both historical events has not been totally elucidated and reconstructed, the status quo and its supporters claim their cultural hegemony over the facts for their favor that liberalism and communism are equally level with danger and impurity.

Although reformasi is the new era of Indonesian politics for more democratic governance, the mainstream of old political culture still plays a dominant role in shaping and directing the political junctures based on paternalistic patronage, pride, and prophecy. On clean from corruption and good governance, Indonesian reformist leaders have to work extra, not simply because the current bureaucratic system is mostly still dominated by the mainstream of the despotic New Order. They do not want to change the old political culture from despotic patrimonial to democratic governance.

About the Author

Dr. Bagoes Wiryomartono is an independent scholar and currently on leave as faculty member at the School of Design, University of Pelita Harapan, Karawaci Tangerang Indonesia. Bagoes obtained his Bachelor’s degree in architecture from Bandung Institute of Technology, Indonesia, and earned his doctorate from Aachen University of Technology in 1990 in Germany. He was a postdoctoral fellow for architecture at the East–West Center in Honolulu, Hawaii, and a Fulbright scholar at the Smithsonian Institution in Washington, DC. The area of specialization of his teaching and research experience is focused on the history, theory, and design of urbanism of various cultures and traditions in Southeast Asia and North America. He was a senior lecturer at the Bandung Institute of Technology (1981–1983, 1993–2002) and a visiting research associate at the Asian Institute, University of Toronto Canada (2003–2005). He was an associate professor at the Department of Architecture, Faculty of Built Environment of Universiti Teknologi Malaysia (2010–2013).
